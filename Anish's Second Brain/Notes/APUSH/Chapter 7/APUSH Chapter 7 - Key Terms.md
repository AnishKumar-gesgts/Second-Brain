# APUSH Chapter 7 - Key Terms

**Textbook:** [[America's History for the AP - Henretta.pdf|America's History for the AP Course]], Chapter 7, "Hammering Out a Federal Republic, 1787-1820."

**Scope:** Only the 23 concepts and events and 8 people in the colored key-terms table at the end of Chapter 7. Page numbers below refer to the textbook's printed pages.

## Key concepts and events

### Building the federal government

**Judiciary Act of 1789 (p. 216)**  
Congress created federal district and circuit courts below the Supreme Court and allowed appeals involving federal law from state courts. The act turned the Constitution's outline of a judiciary into a working national court system. Its provisions later became central to *Marbury v. Madison*.

**Bill of Rights (p. 216)**  
The first ten constitutional amendments, ratified in 1791, protect individual liberties such as speech and religion and guarantee legal protections such as trial by jury. They answered Antifederalist fears that the new national government would threaten citizens' rights, helping legitimate the Constitution. They also left open the larger question of the balance between federal and state power.

**Report on the Public Credit (p. 216)**  
Hamilton's 1790 proposal called for the federal government to pay Confederation securities at face value and assume states' Revolutionary War debts. Reliable repayment would build national credit and make future borrowing possible; federal bonds would also give creditors a stake in the new government. Critics objected that speculators who had bought securities cheaply would profit and that the plan favored wealthy creditors and a lasting national debt. A bargain placing the capital on the Potomac helped Hamilton secure votes for the plan.

**Bank of the United States (p. 218)**  
Hamilton proposed a bank jointly owned by the federal government and private stockholders to hold public funds, issue credit, and lend to merchants. Congress chartered it in 1791. Jefferson and Madison argued that the Constitution did not expressly authorize a bank; Hamilton argued that the **necessary and proper** clause permitted one as a means of carrying out federal powers. The dispute made strict versus broad constitutional interpretation a central party issue.

**Report on Manufactures (p. 218)**  
Hamilton's 1791 report urged support for American manufacturing as part of a stronger, more varied national economy. In this chapter, his immediate tariff proposal emphasized **moderate duties that raised revenue** for interest on the debt and government expenses, rather than tariffs high enough to exclude foreign goods. It contrasted with Jefferson's ideal of a republic grounded in independent farmers.

### Foreign revolutions, neutrality, and parties

**Proclamation of Neutrality (p. 219)**  
Washington declared in 1793 that the United States would not join the war between revolutionary France and Britain. Americans could trade as neutrals, which created profitable shipping opportunities. British interference with that trade showed how difficult it was for the young republic to stay out of European wars.

**French Revolution (p. 219)**  
The upheaval that began in 1789 overthrew France's monarchy and led to radical politics and war across Europe. Americans disagreed over whether its ideals continued their own revolution or its violence threatened order. France's war with Britain also tested U.S. neutrality and deepened Federalist-Republican conflict.

**Jacobins (p. 219)**  
A radical political faction in revolutionary France associated with militant republicanism. Some Americans admired their democratic language and formed political clubs; others feared the violence and attacks on established religion associated with the revolution's radical phase. Reactions to the Jacobins exposed different American views of what a republic should be.

**Whiskey Rebellion (p. 219)**  
In 1794, western Pennsylvania farmers resisted Hamilton's federal excise tax on distilled spirits, including whiskey made from their corn. Washington raised a large militia, and the rebellion dispersed. The response demonstrated that the new federal government could enforce its laws; the protest also showed why western farmers saw Hamilton's financial system as unfair.

**Jay's Treaty (p. 222)**  
Washington sent John Jay to Britain after British seizures of American ships. The 1794 agreement, ratified in 1795, provided for British withdrawal from posts in the Northwest and claims for some ship seizures, while the United States agreed to address debts owed to British creditors. It did not win the neutral-shipping rights many Americans wanted. Republicans attacked it as too favorable to Britain, sharpening the party divide.

**Haitian Revolution (p. 222)**  
Beginning in 1791, enslaved people in the French colony of Saint-Domingue revolted; after prolonged war, slavery there ended and Haiti became independent. Refugees reached U.S. ports, and many American slaveholders feared that successful black liberation would inspire resistance in their own communities. U.S. leaders differed over whether to aid or recognize the revolution, exposing the tension between republican liberty and American slavery.

**XYZ Affair (p. 223)**  
During talks over French seizures of U.S. ships, French intermediaries demanded a loan and bribe from American diplomats. President John Adams identified the agents as X, Y, and Z. The resulting outrage led Congress in 1798 to cut trade with France and authorize attacks on French vessels, producing an **undeclared naval conflict** rather than a formal war.

**Naturalization, Alien, and Sedition Acts (p. 224)**  
Federalists passed these laws in 1798 amid conflict with France and attacks from Republican critics. The Naturalization Act raised the residency requirement for citizenship from **5 to 14 years**; the Alien Act authorized deportation of foreigners; the Sedition Act punished certain published attacks on the president or Congress. Federalist prosecutions of Republican editors and politicians made freedom of speech, immigration, and the legitimacy of party opposition central issues.

**Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (p. 225)**  
Written at the urging of Madison and Jefferson in 1798, these state resolutions declared the Alien and Sedition Acts unconstitutional. They argued that states could judge whether federal laws exceeded constitutional authority. They were a states' rights response to Federalist power and raised the unresolved question of who gets to interpret the Constitution.

### Western land, Jefferson, and war

**Treaty of Greenville (p. 226)**  
After General Anthony Wayne defeated the Western Confederacy at Fallen Timbers in 1794, Native leaders signed this 1795 treaty, ceding extensive land in present-day Ohio while retaining specified lands and rights. It opened more of the region to U.S. settlement and accelerated white migration. The treaty followed years of organized Native resistance, including victories over earlier U.S. armies.

**Marbury v. Madison (1803) (p. 231)**  
The case began when Jefferson's administration withheld a judicial commission made at the end of Adams's presidency. Chief Justice John Marshall ruled that part of the Judiciary Act of 1789 conflicted with the Constitution. The decision asserted **judicial review**: the Supreme Court's authority to declare a congressional law unconstitutional. It strengthened the Court even though Marbury did not receive his commission through the ruling.

**Louisiana Purchase (p. 233)**  
In 1803, the United States bought France's Louisiana territory, securing New Orleans and access to the Mississippi River while greatly expanding U.S. territorial claims. It advanced Jefferson's hopes for land for independent farmers, but he had to accept a broader use of federal power than his earlier strict reading of the Constitution suggested. The purchase did not eliminate Native peoples' claims to the land.

**Embargo Act of 1807 (p. 234)**  
Jefferson barred American ships from leaving U.S. ports to pressure Britain and France to stop restricting American trade. He hoped to defend U.S. neutrality through economic pressure rather than war. The embargo hurt American merchants and exporters badly while failing to make the European powers change course.

**Battle of Tippecanoe (p. 236)**  
In 1811, Indiana territorial governor William Henry Harrison attacked Prophetstown, the community associated with Tecumseh's brother Tenskwatawa, while Tecumseh was away seeking allies. Both sides suffered heavy casualties, and Harrison's forces destroyed the village. The battle weakened the Native confederacy's base and intensified tensions before the War of 1812.

**Treaty of Ghent (p. 241)**  
Signed in December 1814, the treaty ended the War of 1812 and restored prewar U.S.-British borders. It did not resolve impressment in its terms or give either side the territorial gains it had sought. Andrew Jackson's victory at New Orleans occurred in January 1815, **after the treaty was signed but before news of it arrived**.

**McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) (p. 241)**  
Maryland tried to tax a branch of the Second Bank of the United States. Marshall's Court held that Congress could create the bank under its implied **necessary and proper** powers and that a state could not tax a valid federal institution. The ruling affirmed national authority over conflicting state action.

**Adams-Onís Treaty (p. 243)**  
In 1819, Secretary of State John Quincy Adams negotiated Spain's cession of Florida to the United States. In return, the United States accepted Spain's claim to Texas and agreed to a western boundary. The treaty clarified U.S. territorial claims and reduced a major border dispute.

**Monroe Doctrine (p. 244)**  
In 1823, President James Monroe declared that the American continents were closed to further European colonization and said the United States would not interfere in Europe's internal affairs. John Quincy Adams helped shape the policy amid the independence of Latin American republics. It asserted a growing U.S. diplomatic role in the Western Hemisphere, although the declaration came **after** the chapter title's 1820 endpoint.

## Key people

**Alexander Hamilton (p. 216)**  
Washington's treasury secretary. His public-credit plan, national bank, and support for manufacturing aimed to strengthen federal authority and the national economy. Support for and opposition to his program helped create the Federalist and Republican parties.

**Thomas Jefferson (p. 218)**  
Washington's secretary of state, leading opponent of Hamilton's program, and president from 1801 to 1809. He favored an agrarian republic of independent farmers and usually a narrower reading of federal power. As president, he nevertheless kept the national bank and made the Louisiana Purchase, revealing the practical limits of his earlier position.

**John Adams (p. 223)**  
The second president and a Federalist. His administration faced French seizures of U.S. ships, the XYZ Affair, and the divisive Alien and Sedition Acts. Despite pressure for a full war with France, Adams pursued diplomacy that ended the maritime conflict.

**Little Turtle (p. 226)**  
A Miami leader in the Western Confederacy, which resisted U.S. settlement north of the Ohio River. Confederacy warriors defeated U.S. expeditions in 1790 and 1791. His role shows that Native nations organized powerful military resistance to expansion before the U.S. victory at Fallen Timbers and the Treaty of Greenville.

**John Marshall (p. 231)**  
Chief justice beginning in 1801. Through decisions including *Marbury v. Madison* and *McCulloch v. Maryland*, his Court strengthened judicial review and national supremacy. His rulings carried Federalist ideas about federal power and property rights forward after the Federalist Party declined.

**Tecumseh (p. 235)**  
A Shawnee leader who sought a broad Native alliance to stop U.S. settlement. His brother Tenskwatawa supplied a religious message that helped unite followers at Prophetstown. Tecumseh later allied with Britain in the War of 1812 and was killed at the Battle of the Thames in 1813.

**Henry Clay (p. 241)**  
A Kentucky congressman and Speaker of the House who advocated war with Britain. After the War of 1812 he led National Republicans who backed measures such as the Second Bank and federal support for roads. His program shows how some Hamiltonian economic ideas survived inside the Republican Party.

**John Quincy Adams (p. 243)**  
A diplomat who helped negotiate the Treaty of Ghent and served as Monroe's secretary of state. He reached agreements with Britain on Great Lakes naval forces and the northern border, negotiated Florida's transfer from Spain, and helped shape the Monroe Doctrine. His work strengthened U.S. boundaries and diplomatic influence.
