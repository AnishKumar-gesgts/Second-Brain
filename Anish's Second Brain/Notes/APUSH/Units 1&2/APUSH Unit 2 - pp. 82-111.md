## Big picture

From 1660 to 1750, Britain transformed its scattered mainland colonies into a more connected Atlantic empire.

- **Imperial control increased:** The crown created new colonies, imposed the Navigation Acts, and briefly tried to centralize northern colonies in the Dominion of New England.
- **Colonial self-government survived and expanded:** The Glorious Revolution weakened direct royal control, colonial assemblies gained power, and Britain later practiced **salutary neglect**.
- **Atlantic commerce tied the colonies together:** Sugar, tobacco, rice, food, shipping, credit, and manufactured goods connected Britain, Africa, the Caribbean, and mainland North America.
- **Slavery became central to the system:** The South Atlantic economy depended on the forced migration and labor of millions of Africans, while different slave societies developed in the Chesapeake and South Carolina.
- **Native peoples remained major political actors:** Indigenous nations used diplomacy, warfare, trade, and shifting alliances to pursue their own goals during imperial wars.
- **Colonial elites became more powerful:** Southern planters developed a culture of gentility, northern merchants accumulated fortunes, and assemblies increasingly challenged royal governors.

## Restoration colonies and imperial expansion, pp. 82–83

- After the restoration of **Charles II** in 1660, the English crown tried to bring greater order and control to North America.
- Charles II created several new **proprietorships**, colonies granted to individuals or groups who governed them while owing allegiance to the king.
- The Restoration colonies included Carolina, New York, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania.
- Carolina's proprietors attempted to impose a hierarchical, almost feudal society through the Fundamental Constitutions, but settlers—especially in North Carolina—resisted elite control.
- South Carolina developed strong connections with Barbados. Settlers brought plantation agriculture and racial slavery from the Caribbean.
- Early South Carolinians exported food and cattle to the West Indies and participated in the deerskin and Native slave trades. Around 1700, rice became the colony's major plantation crop.
- The demand for rice labor caused a rapid expansion of African slavery. Africans became a majority of South Carolina's population early in the eighteenth century.

### Pennsylvania and the Quakers

- **William Penn** received Pennsylvania from Charles II in 1681 and intended it to be a refuge for **Quakers**.
- Quakers believed that every person possessed an **Inner Light**, a direct connection to God. Their beliefs encouraged spiritual equality, greater religious tolerance, relatively greater equality for women, and pacifism.
- Penn initially purchased land from the Lenni-Lenape rather than simply taking it by force.
- Pennsylvania's Frame of Government protected religious freedom and gave political rights to many property-owning men.
- Penn actively recruited immigrants, including Germans, making Pennsylvania one of the most ethnically and religiously diverse colonies.
- Compared with most other colonies, Pennsylvania began with unusually broad religious toleration and political participation.

## Mercantilism and the Navigation Acts, pp. 83–85

- English leaders embraced **mercantilism**, the idea that government should regulate trade so that commerce strengthened the nation's wealth and military power.
- Parliament's **Navigation Acts** attempted to make the colonies economically useful to England and weaken Dutch commercial competition.
- The 1651 act required colonial trade to use English or colonial ships.
- The 1660 act restricted foreign shipping and required important **enumerated goods**, including tobacco and sugar, to be shipped through England.
- The 1663 act required most European goods bound for the colonies to pass through England first.
- The 1673 Staple Act placed duties on enumerated goods shipped between colonies and strengthened customs enforcement.
- Later acts created vice-admiralty courts and restricted colonial manufacturing in industries that competed with Britain.
- Colonists partly obeyed the system but also smuggled, traded illegally, and resisted enforcement when regulations threatened their profits.
- The Navigation Acts therefore strengthened Britain's Atlantic commercial system without giving the crown complete control over colonial trade.

## The Dominion of New England, pp. 85–86

- Massachusetts Bay strongly resisted imperial trade regulation and defended the independence granted by its old charter.
- In 1679, royal officials separated New Hampshire from Massachusetts.
- In 1684, an English court annulled the Massachusetts Bay charter after royal officials accused the colony of violating the Navigation Acts and suppressing the Church of England.
- **James II**, who became king in 1685, wanted much tighter royal control over the colonies.
- In 1686, the crown combined Massachusetts Bay, Plymouth, Connecticut, and Rhode Island into the **Dominion of New England**. New York and New Jersey were added in 1688.
- James II appointed **Edmund Andros** governor of the Dominion.
- Andros abolished existing legislative assemblies, banned Massachusetts town meetings, promoted Anglican worship, and challenged land titles issued under the old Massachusetts charter.
- Colonists saw the Dominion as an attack on local government, property rights, and religious institutions.

## The Glorious Revolution in England and America, pp. 86–88

- James II alienated English political leaders by rejecting parliamentary limits, practicing Catholicism openly, and behaving like an absolute monarch.
- When James had a Catholic son in 1688, Protestant leaders feared a permanent Catholic dynasty.
- Parliament invited **William of Orange**, a Protestant Dutch prince married to James's Protestant daughter Mary, to intervene.
- James II fled, and William and Mary took the throne in the **Glorious Revolution** of 1688–1689.
- William and Mary accepted the Declaration of Rights, creating a **constitutional monarchy** in which the monarch's power was limited and Parliament—especially the House of Commons—held greater authority.
- Political philosopher **John Locke** defended the revolution in *Two Treatises of Government*.
- Locke argued that legitimate government rests on the **consent of the governed** and that individuals possess natural rights to **life, liberty, and property**.
- Locke's ideas strongly influenced colonial arguments for representative government and later American political thought.

### Colonial rebellions of 1689

- News of the Glorious Revolution triggered uprisings against royal officials in Massachusetts, Maryland, and New York.
- Massachusetts colonists arrested Andros and ended the Dominion of New England.
- The crown did not fully restore the old Puritan system. Massachusetts became a royal colony in 1692, with a royal governor and a broader property-based electorate rather than voting restricted only to church members.
- In Maryland, Protestant colonists overthrew the Catholic proprietary government. The colony temporarily became royal, and the Church of England became established there.
- In New York, **Jacob Leisler** led an uprising against the existing government. A later royal governor had Leisler executed, leaving deep political factionalism in the colony.
- The revolts showed that colonists accepted membership in the empire but expected significant protection for local political rights.

## Imperial wars and Native peoples, pp. 88–90

- Beginning in 1689, Britain and France entered a long era of imperial conflict sometimes called the **Second Hundred Years' War**.
- European wars spread into North America because both empires depended on Native alliances, frontier settlements, and control of Atlantic trade.
- Native peoples were not simply tools of European empires. They chose alliances based on their own diplomatic, territorial, and economic interests.
- Epidemics, warfare, displacement, and migration caused many Native communities to regroup into new political identities, a process the text calls **tribalization**.
- The Five Nations of the Iroquois used their location between British and French territory to preserve influence.
- In 1701, the Iroquois pursued neutrality with France while maintaining the **Covenant Chain**, their diplomatic and trading alliance with New York and later the broader British colonies.
- The Covenant Chain became an important model for British diplomacy with Native nations.
- In the Southeast, Creeks, Choctaws, Cherokees, Yamasees, and other Native peoples made and broke alliances with the British, French, and Spanish as they competed for trade and security.
- The Native slave trade intensified warfare. English-backed raiders captured and sold thousands of Native people into slavery.
- The Tuscarora War drove surviving Tuscaroras north, where they joined the Iroquois and helped create the Six Nations.
- During the Yamasee War of 1715, Native peoples attacked South Carolina after mounting debts and abuses by English traders. South Carolina survived partly by securing Cherokee support.
- In northern conflicts, French-allied Native groups attacked New England settlements, including Deerfield in 1704.
- Britain captured Port Royal in 1710.
- The Treaty of Utrecht in 1713 gave Britain Newfoundland, Acadia, and Hudson Bay from France and the **asiento**, a contract allowing Britain to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America.
- The treaty strengthened Britain's commercial and naval position in the Atlantic.

## The South Atlantic System, pp. 90–93

- The **South Atlantic System** was the interconnected Atlantic economy linking Britain, West Africa, the Caribbean, Brazil, and mainland North America through plantation crops, trade, shipping, credit, and slavery.
- Sugar was the most profitable plantation crop in the Caribbean and Brazil.
- Sugar plantations required enormous investments in land, mills, equipment, and labor. Their profits depended on large enslaved workforces.
- European merchants financed plantations, supplied manufactured goods, transported commodities, and participated in the slave trade.
- British merchants and absentee plantation owners made fortunes from Caribbean sugar.
- By the mid-eighteenth century, reexports of colonial sugar and tobacco made up a major share of British exports.
- Mainland colonies also benefited by supplying the Caribbean with food, lumber, fish, livestock, ships, and commercial services.
- The wealth of the British Atlantic economy therefore rested heavily on plantation slavery even in regions where plantations were uncommon.

## Africa, the slave trade, and the Middle Passage, pp. 93–95

- The Atlantic slave trade expanded dramatically as European plantation economies demanded more labor.
- Portuguese traders transported large numbers of Africans before 1650; Dutch merchants became important later in the seventeenth century; British merchants became dominant during much of the eighteenth century.
- Between 1700 and 1800, British traders transported roughly 2.5 million of the approximately 6.1 million Africans carried across the Atlantic during that century.
- African rulers and merchants participated in the trade, often exchanging captives for firearms and imported goods.
- The slave trade intensified warfare and political competition within Africa and strengthened some states while devastating other communities.
- Because more men than women were exported, the trade also distorted sex ratios in some African regions.
- Captives were marched to coastal trading centers and then forced aboard slave ships.
- The **Middle Passage** was the forced Atlantic voyage of enslaved Africans to the Americas.
- Captives were packed into crowded ships, chained, poorly fed, exposed to disease and dehydration, and subjected to violence and sexual abuse.
- Enslaved people resisted through revolt, refusal to eat, sabotage, and attempts at escape or suicide.
- About one in ten slave voyages experienced some form of revolt, and roughly 14 percent of captives died during the ocean crossing according to the text.

## Plantation slavery in the Americas, pp. 95–97

- Slavery in the Caribbean and Brazil was especially deadly because sugar cultivation required exhausting labor in tropical disease environments.
- Plantation owners treated enslaved people as property and attempted to control their work, movement, family life, and reproduction.
- Colonial laws increasingly made racial slavery permanent and hereditary.

### Chesapeake slavery

- Virginia and Maryland increasingly replaced indentured labor with enslaved African labor during the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries.
- Tobacco work was demanding, but conditions were generally less deadly than sugar or rice cultivation.
- Chesapeake plantations were more dispersed, reducing the spread of some diseases.
- Planters increasingly purchased enslaved women and encouraged reproduction because children inherited enslaved status.
- By the mid-eighteenth century, more than three-quarters of enslaved workers in the Chesapeake were American-born.
- A more balanced sex ratio and natural population growth helped stable families and extended kin networks develop.

### South Carolina slavery

- South Carolina's rice boom after 1700 created a much harsher slave society.
- Planters imported thousands of Africans, including people from regions with experience cultivating rice.
- Africans became a majority of South Carolina's population by the early eighteenth century and reached about 80 percent in some rice-growing districts.
- Rice cultivation in swampy fields exposed workers to mosquitoes, disease, exhaustion, and dangerous irrigation work.
- Death rates remained high and birth rates low, so plantations continued importing Africans.
- Continuous new arrivals kept South Carolina's enslaved population more directly connected to African languages and cultures than the more American-born Chesapeake population.

## African American communities and culture, pp. 97–101

- Enslaved people came from many different African societies and initially identified primarily with particular families, languages, ethnic groups, and homelands rather than with a single "African" identity.
- Planters sometimes deliberately mixed people from different regions because they believed linguistic and cultural differences would make rebellion harder.
- Over time, enslaved people from different backgrounds created new African American communities and shared cultural practices.
- In the Chesapeake, a more balanced sex ratio after about 1725 allowed stronger nuclear families and extended kin networks to emerge, especially on large plantations.
- Family and kin groups preserved names, customs, knowledge, and mutual support across generations.
- African practices survived selectively and blended with new American conditions, producing distinct African American cultures.
- In South Carolina, the large Black majority and continual arrival of African-born people supported especially strong African linguistic and cultural influences.
- Enslaved people created communities despite the constant threat that sale, punishment, and forced separation could destroy families.

### Everyday resistance and rebellion

- Enslaved people resisted slavery in many ways: slowing work, pretending illness, breaking tools, stealing food, running away, preserving independent cultural practices, and occasionally organizing revolts.
- Resistance did not usually destroy the slave system, but it forced owners to devote constant effort to surveillance and punishment.
- Spanish Florida sometimes offered freedom to escaped slaves who reached its territory and accepted Catholicism, giving enslaved people in South Carolina a possible destination for escape.

## Stono Rebellion, pp. 101–102

- In 1739, a group of enslaved people in South Carolina launched the **Stono Rebellion**, the largest slave uprising in the mainland British colonies before the American Revolution.
- The rebels seized weapons and marched toward Spanish Florida, where they hoped to gain freedom.
- Some participants were Portuguese-speaking Catholics from the Kingdom of Kongo and may have had military experience.
- The rebels organized themselves militarily and attracted additional enslaved people as they moved south.
- South Carolina militia defeated the uprising; 44 enslaved rebels were killed in the fighting.
- In response, white South Carolinians temporarily reduced slave imports and imposed tighter controls on enslaved people.
- The rebellion demonstrated both the determination of enslaved people to seek freedom and the insecurity underlying plantation societies with large enslaved majorities.

## The rise of the Southern gentry, pp. 102–103

- As slavery expanded, wealthy planter-merchants became the dominant political and social class in Virginia and South Carolina.
- **William Byrd II** represented this colonial elite. Educated in England, he wanted acceptance among the English gentry but repeatedly encountered prejudice against his colonial status.
- Returning to Virginia, Byrd built a wealthy plantation lifestyle and held a seat on the governor's council.
- Planters used enslaved labor not only to grow tobacco and food but also to produce clothing, shoes, barrels, buildings, and other goods, making plantations increasingly self-sufficient.
- Self-sufficiency helped the Chesapeake elite survive low tobacco prices between about 1670 and 1720.

### White identity, political alliances, and gentility

- After Bacon's Rebellion, Chesapeake elites tried to reduce conflict with poorer white colonists.
- They lowered taxes, expanded opportunities for many white men to vote and hold minor local offices, and encouraged small farmers to participate in the slave economy.
- These policies created political ties between wealthy planters and white yeomen while excluding enslaved Africans from the rights increasingly associated with free white status.
- The planter elite used control of the House of Burgesses to limit royal governors and preserve its own authority.
- At the same time, wealthy planters separated themselves culturally from ordinary whites by adopting **gentility**, a refined lifestyle modeled on the English upper classes.
- Gentry families built brick mansions, purchased imported luxury goods, educated sons in England, and trained daughters in elite manners and social customs.
- The "southern gentlewoman" became an ideal among wealthy planter families.
- The profits produced by enslaved labor allowed the southern gentry to become a stable, educated, and politically powerful ruling class.

## The Northern maritime economy, pp. 103–105

- The South Atlantic economy also transformed New England and the Middle Colonies.
- New England farmers and merchants supplied Caribbean sugar islands with bread, lumber, fish, and meat.
- New York, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania increasingly exported wheat, corn, and flour to the Caribbean.
- By the 1750s, about two-thirds of New England exports and about half of Middle Atlantic exports went to British or French sugar islands.
- **Bills of exchange**, a form of commercial credit, linked the entire system. West Indian planters used London credit to buy enslaved Africans and mainland provisions; mainland merchants then used those bills to buy British manufactured goods.
- Caribbean trade helped create the first major mainland merchant fortunes.
- Northern merchants invested in ships, fishing, rum distilling, sugar refining, and other businesses.
- Boston, Newport, New York, Philadelphia, and other ports became important commercial centers.
- Interior roads, rivers, and wagon routes linked farms to these port cities.
- Colonial cities developed stronger class divisions: wealthy merchants at the top, artisans and shopkeepers in the middle, and laborers, servants, and enslaved people at the bottom.
- Economic downturns hit wage workers and other poor urban residents especially hard.

## The new politics of empire, pp. 105–107

- After 1713, British officials generally governed the colonies less aggressively than James II had.
- Colonial assemblies increasingly controlled taxation and government spending.
- Assemblies often refused to grant governors permanent salaries, forcing royal officials to negotiate with elected representatives.
- Although wealthy men dominated most assemblies, a relatively broad group of property-owning white men could vote by contemporary standards.
- Crowds, petitions, local elections, and public opinion could also pressure political leaders.

### Salutary neglect and patronage

- Under George I and George II, Britain followed a looser colonial policy known as **salutary neglect**.
- **Robert Walpole**, Britain's leading minister from 1720 to 1742, believed profitable colonial trade mattered more than strict enforcement of every imperial rule.
- As long as colonial commerce benefited Britain, many violations of trade regulations were tolerated.
- Walpole also relied heavily on **patronage**, granting government offices and salaries to political supporters.
- Patronage often produced weak or self-interested imperial officials in America.
- Colonists increasingly associated British "court" politics with corruption and contrasted it with the representative authority of their own assemblies.
- Salutary neglect therefore encouraged colonial economic growth while allowing habits of self-government to deepen.

## Protecting the mercantile system, pp. 107–110

- Britain still acted forcefully when officials believed imperial security or major commercial interests were threatened.
- Georgia was founded in 1732 partly as a military buffer protecting South Carolina from Spanish Florida.
- Commercial rivalry with Spain contributed to the **War of Jenkins' Ear** beginning in 1739.
- British expeditions against Spanish Florida and Cartagena achieved little and caused heavy losses.
- The conflict merged into the wider War of the Austrian Succession.
- In 1745, New England forces captured the French fortress of Louisbourg, but Britain returned it to France in the 1748 Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle.
- Many colonists resented the decision because it showed that British leaders might sacrifice colonial gains for broader imperial goals.

### Trade and manufacturing restrictions

- Parliament continued passing laws intended to protect British producers from colonial competition.
- The **Woolen Act** of 1699 restricted colonial textile exports.
- The **Hat Act** of 1732 limited colonial hat production and export.
- The **Iron Act** of 1750 restricted manufacture of finished iron goods in the colonies while encouraging shipment of raw iron to Britain.
- The **Molasses Act** of 1733 placed a high tariff on molasses imported from the French West Indies.
- Because New England merchants depended heavily on cheap French molasses for rum production, they widely evaded the law through smuggling and bribery.
- Colonial resistance showed the limits of Britain's ability to enforce mercantilist policy.

### Paper money and land banks

- A shortage of gold and silver led colonies to experiment with paper currency.
- **Land banks** were colonial institutions that issued loans in paper money, usually backed by borrowers' land.
- Supporters believed paper money expanded credit and helped farmers and merchants.
- British merchants feared being repaid in depreciated colonial currency.
- The Currency Act of 1751 restricted new land banks in New England and limited the use of public paper money for private debts.
- By the late 1740s and early 1750s, some British leaders were again considering stricter imperial supervision, suggesting that the era of salutary neglect would not last indefinitely.

## Must-know terms and people

- **Proprietorship:** Colony granted by the crown to an individual or group with broad governing authority.
- **Quakers:** Protestant group emphasizing the Inner Light, spiritual equality, pacifism, and religious toleration.
- **Navigation Acts:** English laws regulating colonial shipping and trade to benefit the empire and weaken foreign competitors.
- **Dominion of New England:** James II's centralized royal colony combining several northern colonies from 1686 to 1689.
- **Glorious Revolution:** 1688–1689 overthrow of James II and accession of William and Mary.
- **Constitutional monarchy:** Monarchy in which the ruler's authority is limited by law and representative institutions.
- **Second Hundred Years' War:** Long period of British-French imperial warfare beginning in 1689 and continuing through the eighteenth century.
- **Tribalization:** Formation or reorganization of Native political communities in response to disease, migration, warfare, and European colonization.
- **Covenant Chain:** Diplomatic and trading alliance linking the Iroquois first with New York and later with the broader British colonies.
- **South Atlantic System:** Atlantic economic network connecting plantation production, slavery, shipping, credit, and trade among Europe, Africa, the Caribbean, Brazil, and mainland North America.
- **Middle Passage:** Forced transatlantic voyage carrying enslaved Africans to the Americas.
- **Stono Rebellion:** 1739 South Carolina uprising in which enslaved people attempted to reach freedom in Spanish Florida.
- **Gentility:** Refined upper-class lifestyle and social behavior modeled on the English gentry.
- **Salutary neglect:** British policy of loose enforcement of colonial regulations as long as the colonies remained profitable and loyal.
- **Patronage:** Distribution of government offices, salaries, or favors to political supporters.
- **Land banks:** Colonial institutions that issued paper-money loans backed primarily by land.
- **William Penn:** Quaker proprietor and founder of Pennsylvania who promoted religious toleration and negotiated early land purchases with Native peoples.
- **Edmund Andros:** Royal governor of the Dominion of New England whose authoritarian policies provoked colonial resistance.
- **William of Orange:** Protestant Dutch prince who became King William III after the Glorious Revolution.
- **John Locke:** Political philosopher who argued for natural rights, consent of the governed, and the right to resist illegitimate government.
- **Jacob Leisler:** New York merchant who led a 1689 uprising during the Glorious Revolution and was later executed.
- **William Byrd II:** Wealthy Virginia planter whose career illustrated the rise and culture of the southern gentry.
- **Robert Walpole:** Leading British minister from 1720 to 1742 associated with patronage and salutary neglect toward the colonies.

## Likely quiz comparisons

- **Chesapeake slavery vs. South Carolina slavery:** Chesapeake tobacco was less deadly, populations increasingly grew naturally, and stronger family networks developed; South Carolina rice cultivation was more lethal, required continued African imports, and preserved stronger direct African cultural influence.
- **James II's imperial policy vs. salutary neglect:** James II tried direct centralization through the Dominion of New England; later ministers allowed broad colonial autonomy as long as trade remained profitable.
- **Dominion of New England vs. colonial assemblies:** The Dominion abolished local representative institutions, while the post-Glorious Revolution era saw assemblies gain greater control over taxes, salaries, and policy.
- **Native alliances vs. European imperial goals:** Britain and France sought Native military support, but Native nations changed alliances and pursued their own territorial, commercial, and diplomatic objectives.
- **Southern plantation economy vs. Northern maritime economy:** The South produced tobacco and rice with enslaved labor; northern colonies profited through food exports, shipping, fishing, distilling, commerce, and services tied to the same Atlantic plantation system.
- **Mercantilism vs. colonial economic behavior:** Britain tried to channel trade for imperial benefit, but colonists regularly smuggled, bribed officials, and resisted rules that interfered with profitable Atlantic commerce.
- **Political inclusion vs. racial exclusion:** More white men gained voting rights and local political roles at the same time that hereditary racial slavery became more entrenched.
