## Big picture

- Before Europeans arrived, North America contained large, complex societies, including the **Mississippian mound builders at Cahokia** and the **Ancestral Puebloans (Anasazi)**.
- European colonization was driven by **God, gold, and glory** and transformed both hemispheres through conquest, conversion, settlement, trade, forced labor, slavery, and the **Columbian Exchange**.
- The major colonial powers developed different patterns:
  - **Spain:** mining, missions, presidios, and coerced Native labor.
  - **France:** fur trading, forts, Jesuit missions, and comparatively collaborative Native alliances.
  - **England:** large-scale settlement, agriculture, representative assemblies, mercantilism, and eventually plantation slavery.
- English colonies developed strong regional differences: commercial and family-centered New England, grain-producing Middle Colonies, and plantation-based Chesapeake and Southern Colonies.
- Colonial resistance existed long before the American Revolution, including **Popé’s Rebellion**, **Bacon’s Rebellion**, and the **Stono Rebellion**.
- The lecture especially emphasizes the **Chesapeake/Virginia**, **Massachusetts Bay**, and **Pennsylvania**.

## Native societies, Columbus, and the Columbian Exchange

- Important pre-Columbian societies included the Mississippi mound builders at **Cahokia** and the **Ancestral Puebloans**.
- **Leif Erikson** reached Newfoundland around 1000.
- **Christopher Columbus**, sailing for Ferdinand and Isabella of Spain, reached the Caribbean in 1492 and made four voyages.
- Spanish colonization devastated the **Arawaks/Taínos** through warfare, mining, forced labor, and disease—especially smallpox.
- The **Columbian Exchange** moved organisms, goods, people, and diseases between the Americas and Afro-Eurasia.
  - Americas received grains, citrus fruits, horses, and cattle.
  - Afro-Eurasia received corn, potatoes, tomatoes, chocolate, and tobacco.
- Sugarcane originated in India and coffee in Ethiopia, but Europeans cultivated both as Caribbean cash crops.
- European contact also expanded the transatlantic slave trade.

## Spain and Portugal in the Americas

- European imperialists pursued **God, gold, and glory**.
- The **Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)** divided claims in the Americas between Spain and Portugal; Portugal received territory that became Brazil while Spain claimed most of the remainder.
- **Hernán Cortés** conquered the Aztec Empire in present-day Mexico.
- **Francisco Pizarro** conquered the Inca Empire in present-day Peru.
- After roughly 300 years of colonialism, Latin American independence leaders included **Miguel Hidalgo**, a Mexican priest, and **Simón Bolívar**, a South American creole.
- In late-eighteenth-century California, **Father Junípero Serra** established 21 missions along El Camino Real for conversion and forced Native labor.
- **Gaspar de Portolá**, a Spanish military leader and governor, established presidios from San Diego to Monterey to secure Spanish territory.

## New France and later French influence

- In the late seventeenth century, **René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle** traveled the Mississippi and claimed Louisiana for France, naming it for Louis XIV.
- French explorers and settlers often included **Jesuits**, whose missions sought to convert Native peoples.
- France’s main economic goal was the fur trade, especially beaver pelts; forts and missions supported that trade.
- Because French colonies depended on commerce more than dense agricultural settlement, French relations with Native nations—especially the **Hurons**—were generally more collaborative than Spanish or English relations.
- France and Spain promoted less mass migration to North America than England did.
- In 1803, **Napoleon Bonaparte** sold Louisiana to President **Thomas Jefferson** while occupied by European wars and challenged by Caribbean slave revolt, especially the Haitian Revolution led by **Toussaint L’Ouverture**.
- On May 5, 1862, Napoleon III’s attempt to control Mexico suffered a major setback at the Battle of Puebla.

## Virginia and the Chesapeake

- **Sir Walter Raleigh** named Virginia for Queen Elizabeth I, the “Virgin Queen.”
- England’s first attempted settlement, **Roanoke**, disappeared.
- **Jamestown**, founded in 1607 by the joint-stock Virginia Company and named for James I, survived after severe early hardship.
- Settlers endured the **Starving Time**, disease, hunger, and conflict with the Powhatan peoples.
- By 1619, **John Rolfe** had made tobacco profitable, married Pocahontas as part of a temporary peace, and joined the **House of Burgesses**.
- John Rolfe, not John Smith, developed Virginia’s profitable tobacco business.
- The hot, humid Chesapeake climate promoted disease. A mostly male population also suffered from poor nutrition and hygiene.
- Virginia and Maryland formed the Chesapeake region, whose economy became centered on tobacco and plantation labor.

## Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay

### Plymouth

- In 1620, **Miles Standish**, **William Bradford**, and the Pilgrims/Separatists sailed on the Mayflower and founded Plymouth.
- They migrated mainly for religious freedom rather than immediate profit.
- The **Mayflower Compact** established an agreement for self-government.
- Assistance from the Wampanoags, including **Tisquantum/Squanto**, helped the colony survive; the colonists later celebrated the first Thanksgiving.
- Plymouth later fought in **King Philip’s/Metacom’s War (1675–1676)**.

### Massachusetts Bay

- In 1630, **John Winthrop** and the Puritans sailed on the Arbella and founded Massachusetts Bay.
- Pilgrims were **Separatists** who wanted to leave the Church of England.
- Puritans wanted to reform the Anglican Church by creating an exemplary community—a **“city upon a hill.”**
- Massachusetts Bay fought in the **Pequot War (1636–1638)**.
- Compared with the Chesapeake, Cape Cod/New England was cleaner, more family-oriented, and healthier.
- Puritan religious conformity contributed to the Salem witch trials, in which 20 people were executed.

## Reasons colonies were founded and changes of control

- **Maryland**, led by Lord Baltimore, became a refuge for Catholics.
- **Pennsylvania**, led by William Penn, became a refuge for Quakers.
- **Rhode Island**, led by Roger Williams, protected religious dissenters such as Anne Hutchinson.
- **Georgia**, led by James Oglethorpe, was associated with debtors and served as a southern buffer.
- The **Carolinas** were founded by proprietors connected to Barbados planters; philosopher **John Locke** helped draft the Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina.
- **New Netherland (1614–1664)** became New York after England conquered the Dutch colony governed by Peter Stuyvesant.
- New Amsterdam on Manhattan became New York City.
- Swedish colonists introduced log-cabin architecture in the Delaware Valley.
- Russia began colonizing Alaska in the mid-eighteenth century.

## Mercantilism, regional economies, and colonial government

- Spain, France, and England practiced **mercantilism**, using colonies to provide wealth and resources to the mother country.
  - New Spain: gold and silver mining.
  - New France: beaver and other furs.
  - New England Colonies: lumber, ships, and textiles.
  - Middle Colonies: grains.
  - Southern Colonies: cash crops such as tobacco and indigo.
- England often governed through **salutary neglect**, allowing considerable colonial self-government while trade remained profitable.
- Royal governors and appointed upper houses represented imperial authority; elected lower houses/assemblies and juries developed more distinct colonial interests.
- In 1735, a New York jury acquitted journalist **John Peter Zenger** of seditious libel against Governor William Cosby, supporting the principle of press freedom.

## Labor, land, and slavery

- **Indentured servants** exchanged roughly four to seven years of labor for passage, room, and board.
- The **headright system** granted land to colonists who paid for workers’ transportation, helping wealthy elites accumulate land.
- Indentured servants initially outnumbered enslaved laborers in the English colonial workforce.
- By around 1750, elites increasingly preferred permanently enslaved African labor over upwardly mobile white servants whom they considered politically threatening.
- Britain abolished participation in the transatlantic slave trade in 1807, and the United States prohibited the importation of enslaved people beginning in 1808.
- Most enslaved Africans transported across the Atlantic went to the Caribbean and Brazil; only a small share went directly to mainland British North America/the future United States.
- The **Middle Passage** was the forced Atlantic voyage of enslaved Africans to the Americas.

## Albany Plan, imperial war, and the road toward resistance

- **Benjamin Franklin** published the segmented-snake **“Join, or Die”** cartoon in the Pennsylvania Gazette.
- Franklin used it to support colonial unity through the **Albany Plan of Union**, which the colonies rejected.
- It also represented the need to fight together with Britain during the **Seven Years’ War/French and Indian War (1754–1763)**.
- Britain defeated France, but Native nations around the Great Lakes resisted British expansion in **Pontiac’s Rebellion**.
- The **Proclamation of 1763** barred settlement west of the proclamation line.
- Parliament imposed additional taxes to pay imperial expenses.
- Many colonists continued moving west and protested **“no taxation without representation.”**

## Rebellions before the American Revolution

- **Popé’s Rebellion/Pueblo Revolt (1680):** Pueblo peoples rose against Spanish rule in New Mexico. Spain later reconquered the region.
- **Bacon’s Rebellion (1676):** Land-hungry Virginia settlers led by Nathaniel Bacon fought Native peoples, including the Doeg and Susquehannock, and rebelled against Governor William Berkeley. Bacon died of dysentery.
- **Stono Rebellion (1739):** Enslaved Kongolese people rebelled against South Carolina plantation owners and attempted to reach Spanish Florida. The revolt was defeated, and colonial authorities imposed harsher slave restrictions.

## Must-know terms and people

- **Columbian Exchange:** Transfer of plants, animals, people, diseases, and goods between the Americas and Afro-Eurasia.
- **God, gold, and glory:** Religious, economic, and political motives for European imperialism.
- **Mercantilism:** Economic system in which colonies supplied resources and trade was regulated to enrich the mother country.
- **Joint-stock company:** Business funded by multiple investors who shared risk and profit; the Virginia Company funded Jamestown.
- **House of Burgesses:** Virginia’s elected representative assembly, first convened in 1619.
- **Mayflower Compact:** Agreement by Plymouth settlers to create a self-governing political community.
- **Separatists/Pilgrims:** Protestants who wanted to leave the Church of England.
- **Puritans:** Protestants who wanted to reform the Church of England from within.
- **City upon a hill:** Winthrop’s image of Massachusetts Bay as a model Christian community.
- **Salutary neglect:** Britain’s loose enforcement of colonial regulations, which allowed colonial self-government to grow.
- **Indentured servant:** Worker bound to labor for a set term in return for passage and support.
- **Headright:** Land awarded for paying an immigrant’s transportation.
- **Middle Passage:** Forced voyage carrying enslaved Africans across the Atlantic.
- **Albany Plan of Union:** Franklin’s unsuccessful proposal for unified colonial government and defense.
- **Proclamation of 1763:** British restriction on colonial settlement west of the Appalachian boundary after the French and Indian War.
- **John Rolfe:** Virginia planter who developed profitable tobacco cultivation and married Pocahontas.
- **William Bradford:** Plymouth governor.
- **John Winthrop:** Massachusetts Bay governor who described the colony as a city upon a hill.
- **William Penn:** Quaker proprietor and founder of Pennsylvania.
- **Roger Williams:** Religious dissenter and founder of Rhode Island.
- **John Peter Zenger:** Printer acquitted of seditious libel in 1735.
- **Nathaniel Bacon:** Leader of the 1676 Virginia rebellion.
- **Popé:** Pueblo religious leader who organized the 1680 revolt.
- **Toussaint L’Ouverture:** Major leader of the Haitian Revolution.

## Likely stimulus-based comparisons

- **Spanish vs. French colonization:** Spain emphasized conquest, mining, missions, and coerced labor; France emphasized the fur trade, forts, missions, and Native alliances.
- **French vs. English settlement:** French colonization involved fewer migrants and relied on trade networks; English colonies attracted larger settler populations and displaced Native peoples more extensively.
- **Chesapeake vs. New England:** The Chesapeake was hot, unhealthy, male-dominated, dispersed, and tobacco-based; New England was healthier, family-oriented, town-centered, and shaped by Puritan religion.
- **Plymouth vs. Massachusetts Bay:** Plymouth’s Separatists left the Church of England; Massachusetts Bay’s Puritans hoped to reform it by example.
- **Indentured servitude vs. racial slavery:** Indenture was temporary and offered eventual freedom; slavery became permanent, hereditary, and racially defined.
- **Popé’s, Bacon’s, and Stono Rebellions:** Popé’s was Native resistance to Spain; Bacon’s joined frontier violence with class conflict against Virginia’s elite; Stono was an enslaved uprising against South Carolina slavery.
- **Colonial assemblies vs. imperial government:** Governors and appointed upper houses represented the crown, while elected lower houses defended local colonial interests.
- **Albany Plan vs. colonial reality:** Franklin promoted unity for defense, but the separate colonies rejected centralized government.

## Standard APUSH corrections to the lecture sheet

- The **Treaty of Tordesillas** was negotiated by Spain and Portugal with papal support; it was not simply a territorial ruling issued by Pope Alexander VI.
- The Salem witch trials are conventionally dated **1692–1693**, with the executions occurring in 1692.
- **New Sweden** was centered in the Delaware River valley, not the Hudson River valley.
- Britain ended its slave trade in **1807**; the U.S. prohibition on importing enslaved people took effect in **1808**.
- Cotton did not become the South’s dominant export crop until after the cotton gin and westward expansion; for the colonial era, emphasize **tobacco, rice, and indigo**.
- Standard accounts usually estimate that about **4–5 percent** of Africans carried across the Atlantic arrived directly in mainland British North America/the United States, not 8 percent.
