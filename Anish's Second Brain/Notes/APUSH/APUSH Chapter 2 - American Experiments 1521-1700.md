## Big picture

By 1700, three colonial patterns had developed:

- **Spanish tribute colonies:** Spain took over Indigenous tribute and labor systems in Mexico and the Andes and extracted silver.
- **Plantation colonies:** Tropical colonies grew export crops such as sugar and tobacco with increasingly coercive labor, especially African slavery.
- **Neo-European colonies:** Temperate colonies reproduced European family farming, towns, religious communities, and local government.

All three were shaped by disease, ecological change, Native resistance, European religious conflict, and competition for land and labor.

## Spain and the Columbian Exchange, pp. 42–44

- After Cortés conquered the Aztecs and Pizarro defeated the Incas, Spanish conquerors received **encomiendas**, which allowed them to collect Indigenous labor and tribute.
- Spanish officials adapted the Inca **mita** labor draft for silver mining.
- Major silver mines included Zacatecas, Guanajuato, and Potosí. Potosí produced enormous wealth and connected the Americas to trade with China and Europe.
- Silver strengthened Spain at first but contributed to inflation and long-term economic weakness.
- Spanish America developed a mixed society: **mestizos** (Spanish-Indigenous), **mulattos** (Spanish-African), and **zambos** (Indigenous-African). The **casta system** ranked racial groups legally and socially.
- Spaniards established **haciendas**, or large estates. Indigenous people remained the majority in Mexico and Peru, often living in Native communities under Native leaders.
- Catholic conversion was widespread, but Indigenous communities reshaped Catholic practices and created distinct forms of Native Christianity.

### Columbian Exchange

- The **Columbian Exchange** was the movement of plants, animals, people, and diseases between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres.
- Smallpox, measles, influenza, yellow fever, and other Eurasian/African diseases devastated Native populations that lacked immunity. In many densely populated areas, populations fell by 90 percent or more.
- Syphilis was the major disease carried from the Americas to Europe.
- American crops—especially maize, potatoes, manioc, sweet potatoes, and tomatoes—improved diets and increased population growth in Europe, Africa, and Asia.
- Europeans introduced cattle, pigs, horses, oxen, chickens, honeybees, wheat, barley, rye, and rice, transforming American landscapes.

## Protestant challenge to Spain, pp. 44–46

- Spain's American wealth made it Europe's leading Catholic power, but Protestant opposition and imperial rivalry weakened its control.
- The Dutch provinces revolted against Spanish Catholic rule and formed the Dutch Republic in 1581.
- Henry VIII broke with Rome in 1534 and created the Church of England. Elizabeth I established a Protestant settlement while retaining some Catholic-style practices.
- **Francis Drake** attacked Spanish shipping and completed the first English circumnavigation of the globe.
- Philip II sent the **Spanish Armada** in 1588 to defeat England and restore Catholic power. English ships and storms destroyed the fleet.
- Spain spent American wealth on religious wars, suffered inflation and high taxes, and lost population through emigration.
- England's population and manufacturing grew. Its **outwork** textile system produced cloth in rural households.
- **Mercantilism** used state support, trade restrictions, and export growth to increase national wealth and power. England's stronger economy helped fund colonization.

## Plantation colonies, pp. 46–56

### Brazil, sugar, and tobacco

- Portugal developed sugar plantations in Brazil, especially in Pernambuco and Bahia.
- Sugarcane had to be processed near the fields, so plantations combined farming, milling, and refining.
- Smallpox devastated coastal Indigenous populations, causing Portuguese planters to rely increasingly on African slaves. By 1620, the transition was essentially complete.
- England's early attempts at colonization failed, including the “lost colony” of Roanoke.
- In 1607, the Virginia Company founded Jamestown. The first settlers were mostly male adventurers seeking quick wealth, not farmers building a stable community.
- Jamestown suffered from poor location, lack of fresh water, disease, hunger, and failure to grow enough food.

### Virginia, tobacco, and Maryland

- Tobacco became Virginia's profitable cash crop and created a land-intensive plantation economy.
- The **House of Burgesses**, first convened in 1619, was an elected assembly that could make laws and levy taxes, although officials in England could veto its acts.
- The **headright system** gave land to people who paid for immigrants' passage. It encouraged migration but allowed wealthy planters to accumulate large estates.
- Riverfront plantations dominated the Chesapeake because rivers made it easier to ship tobacco and receive supplies. Few towns developed.
- In 1622, **Opechancanough** coordinated an attack by twelve Native chiefdoms that killed 347 English settlers. English retaliation seized Native fields, declared perpetual war, and sold some captives into slavery.
- In 1624, James I revoked the Virginia Company's charter and made Virginia a **royal colony**. The king appointed the governor, but the House of Burgesses survived.
- **Lord Baltimore** founded Maryland in 1634 as a refuge for Catholics. Protestant settlers and Catholics shared the colony, and the **Toleration Act of 1649** granted Christians the right to practice their religion.
- Maryland's tobacco economy made it resemble Virginia despite its different religious purpose.

### Caribbean plantations and labor

- English, French, and Dutch colonies spread through the Caribbean. Many Native populations were displaced or destroyed.
- From the 1640s, planters shifted from tobacco and other crops to sugar in Barbados, Jamaica, Nevis, and Martinique.
- Plantations grew from small family farms into large estates as planters consolidated land and labor.
- Chesapeake tobacco exports rose from 3 million pounds in 1640 to 10 million pounds in 1660.
- Plantation life was dangerous because of malaria, yellow fever, isolation, and high death rates.

### Indentured servitude and African slavery

- **Indentured servants** signed contracts to work for four or five years in exchange for passage to America. Afterward, they could theoretically work independently and acquire land.
- Servants were often exploited through long hours, beatings, contract extensions, restrictions on marriage, and sale of contracts.
- In the Chesapeake, about half of male servants died before completing their terms; only about one-quarter gained property and respectability.
- Caribbean sugar plantations needed more workers than indentured migration could provide. Barbados adopted comprehensive slave legislation in 1661.
- Africans arrived in Virginia in 1619. Their population grew gradually: about 400 lived in the Chesapeake in 1649, around 2 percent of the population, and about 5 percent by 1670.
- Some Africans initially gained freedom through baptism, purchase, escape, or court action. **Elizabeth Key** successfully won her freedom in court.
- As tobacco prices collapsed, planters sought cheaper permanent labor. Colonial law increasingly defined Africans as legally inferior and made slavery hereditary.
- By the late seventeenth century, racial categories increasingly replaced religion as the basis for legal status.

## Neo-European colonies, pp. 56–66

### New France

- **Samuel de Champlain** founded Quebec in 1608. New France focused on the fur trade and Catholic missions rather than dense settlement.
- French traders worked with the Montagnais, Micmacs, Ottawas, Ojibwas, and Hurons. Beaver pelts were especially valuable.
- Jesuit missionaries learned Native languages and lived among Native communities, but disease and failed prayers weakened their influence.
- In 1662, New France became a royal colony. French indentured contracts were generally more generous than English ones.
- Migration remained low because France was heavily populated, military service was required, Huguenots were discouraged from migrating, and peasants had strong ties to village lands.
- New France claimed a vast interior through explorers, forts, missionaries, and trading communities. Marquette reached the Mississippi in 1673; La Salle claimed Louisiana in 1681.

### New Netherland and the Iroquois

- Dutch merchants dominated European banking, shipping, and trade.
- Henry Hudson explored the Northeast in 1609. The Dutch founded Fort Orange in 1614 and New Netherland in 1621, with New Amsterdam on Manhattan.
- The colony remained small because the Dutch population was limited and many migrants preferred opportunities in Asia.
- England conquered New Netherland in 1664 and renamed it New York.
- The Five Nations of the **Iroquois** used their location between French and Dutch colonies to gain guns, goods, and influence.
- In the **Beaver Wars**, the Iroquois attacked neighboring Native peoples, including the Hurons, Neutrals, Eries, and Susquehannocks, seeking control of the fur trade and territory.
- The wars displaced Native peoples and reshaped northeastern diplomacy. The Iroquois later allied with the English.

### New England and Puritan government

- The **Pilgrims** were separatist Puritans who founded Plymouth in 1620.
- Without a royal charter for their exact settlement, they created the **Mayflower Compact**, agreeing to self-government.
- Plymouth developed representative government, property ownership, and religious freedom of conscience.
- In 1630, **John Winthrop** led 900 Puritans to Massachusetts Bay. He described the colony as a “City upon a Hill,” a model Christian society watched by the world.
- Massachusetts Bay limited voting and officeholding to male church members, established Puritanism as the official religion, and used the Bible as a legal guide.
- Puritans emphasized **predestination**, the belief that God had already chosen the few who would be saved.
- **Roger Williams** opposed an established church and argued that government should not control individual belief. Banished in 1636, he founded Providence; Rhode Island later guaranteed religious freedom.
- **Anne Hutchinson** emphasized direct revelation and the **covenant of grace**, arguing that salvation came through God's grace rather than a **covenant of works** based on good behavior. She was banished in 1637.
- Connecticut developed self-government and allowed most property-owning men to vote, making it somewhat more inclusive than Massachusetts Bay.

### Puritan society and witchcraft

- Puritans believed unusual events could reveal God's or Satan's power. New England authorities executed people for witchcraft before the Salem crisis.
- In 1692, Salem courts accepted “spectral evidence,” and 19 people were executed.
- Historians connect the crisis to local rivalries, social and economic conflict, gender expectations, political instability, and fear of Native attacks.
- After Salem, officials discouraged witchcraft prosecutions and educated people increasingly explained events through natural causes, reflecting the influence of the Enlightenment.
- New England's **town meeting** gave property-holding men power over taxes, roads, local rules, and officials.
- New England had broader landownership and more political participation than the Chesapeake, but it was not fully equal: wealthy men received larger plots and controlled many offices.

## Conflict and instability, pp. 66–72

### Pequot War, 1636–1637

- Native groups formed alliances for trade and defense. The Pequots' alliance with the Dutch made them competitors of English traders.
- After violence involving English traders, Massachusetts and Connecticut forces attacked a Pequot village in 1637 and killed about 500 people.
- English settlers divided Pequot lands. Puritans interpreted the victory as evidence that God favored English expansion.
- Missionaries such as John Eliot created praying towns, but few Native people became full members of Puritan churches.

### Metacom's War, 1675–1676

- By the 1670s, English settlers outnumbered Native people in New England three to one. Disease, land loss, and economic pressure had devastated Native communities.
- **Metacom**, the Wampanoag leader also called King Philip, resisted English expansion.
- Disputes over trade, English livestock destroying Native crops, land, and the execution of three Native men after the death of Sassamon increased tensions.
- Metacom allied with the Narragansetts and Nipmucks and attacked English settlements.
- The war killed about 1,000 English settlers and 4,500 Native people. English forces eventually killed Metacom in 1676.
- The war did not remove all Native people from New England, but it largely destroyed the political independence of the Wampanoag, Narragansett, and Nipmuck peoples in the region.

### Bacon's Rebellion, 1676

- Virginia's planter elite controlled nearly half of the settled land, held political offices, and benefited from Governor **William Berkeley's** patronage.
- Tobacco prices fell, taxes rose, and landless former servants struggled to acquire farms.
- Poor settlers wanted war against Native peoples and access to frontier land; Berkeley preferred trade and a controlled labor force.
- **Nathaniel Bacon** raised an army after Berkeley denied him a military commission. Bacon's followers forced elections and reforms, then burned Jamestown.
- Bacon died of dysentery. Berkeley retook control and executed 23 rebels.
- After the rebellion, Virginia leaders appeased poorer whites while planters increasingly relied on enslaved Africans. The shift toward hereditary racial slavery reduced conflict among free whites but created a permanent system of racial exploitation.

## Must-know terms and people

- **Encomienda:** Spanish grant to collect Indigenous labor and tribute.
- **Mita:** Inca labor draft appropriated by the Spanish.
- **Columbian Exchange:** Movement of organisms, people, and diseases between hemispheres.
- **Mercantilism:** State-supported trade system designed to strengthen national wealth and power.
- **House of Burgesses:** Virginia's elected assembly.
- **Royal colony:** Colony governed through officials appointed by the monarch.
- **Headright system:** Land grants for financing immigrants' passage.
- **Indentured servitude:** Temporary contract labor exchanged for passage.
- **Chattel slavery:** Hereditary system treating enslaved people as property.
- **Pilgrims:** Separatist Puritans who founded Plymouth.
- **Puritans:** Protestants seeking to reform the Church of England.
- **Predestination:** Calvinist belief that God has already chosen who will be saved.
- **Covenant of grace:** Salvation through God's grace.
- **Town meeting:** Local New England government based on votes by property-holding men.
- **Philip II:** Catholic Spanish king who fought Protestant rivals.
- **Francis Drake:** English privateer who attacked Spanish shipping.
- **Opechancanough:** Powhatan successor who led the 1622 attack.
- **Lord Baltimore:** Catholic proprietor of Maryland.
- **John Winthrop:** Governor and religious leader of Massachusetts Bay.
- **Roger Williams:** Founder of Providence and advocate of religious freedom.
- **Anne Hutchinson:** Massachusetts dissenter who emphasized the covenant of grace.
- **Metacom:** Wampanoag leader of Metacom's War.
- **Nathaniel Bacon:** Leader of Bacon's Rebellion.

## Likely quiz comparisons

- **Spanish tribute colonies vs. plantation colonies:** Both used coercive labor and extracted wealth, but Spanish colonies centered on tribute and silver while plantation colonies centered on export crops.
- **Plantation colonies vs. neo-European colonies:** Plantations were tropical, export-oriented, labor-intensive, and socially hierarchical; neo-European colonies were temperate, more family-based, and organized around farms, towns, religion, and local government.
- **New France vs. New England:** New France had a small settler population, fur trade, Catholic missions, and extensive Native alliances; New England had family migration, Puritan institutions, town government, and denser settlement.
- **Metacom's War vs. Bacon's Rebellion:** Metacom's War was Native resistance to English expansion; Bacon's Rebellion was an internal uprising of colonists against Virginia's planter elite, triggered partly by Native policy.
- **Indentured servants vs. enslaved Africans:** Both supplied plantation labor, but indenture was temporary and theoretically ended in freedom, while racial slavery became permanent and hereditary.
