## Big picture

After the French and Indian War, Britain tried to pay for and administer its enlarged empire. Colonial resistance moved from objections to particular taxes toward a dispute over **sovereignty**: Parliament claimed complete authority over the colonies, while Patriots claimed that colonial assemblies had equal authority over internal affairs. British coercion, western land conflicts, and new Patriot organizations turned that constitutional crisis into independence by 1776.

- The Great War for Empire left Britain with a huge debt, a larger North American territory, and a permanent military presence to fund.
- Colonists resisted new taxes, customs enforcement, and troop deployments because they believed these threatened English liberties, local self-government, and economic opportunity.
- Boycotts and crowd action forced Britain to retreat more than once, but each new assertion of parliamentary supremacy hardened Patriot resistance.
- The Tea Act and Coercive Acts made a Massachusetts dispute continental; the Continental Congress created intercolonial cooperation, and fighting at Lexington and Concord made compromise much harder.
- By 1776, **Common Sense** and the Declaration of Independence reframed the conflict as a republican revolution based on natural rights and popular sovereignty.

## Britain remakes the empire, pp. 152-156

### The costs of empire

- The 1756-1763 Great War for Empire raised Britain’s national debt from about £75 million to £133 million; interest consumed about 60% of the national budget.
- British ministers believed the colonies should help pay for the army and administration needed to defend Canada, frontier forts, and the expanded empire.
- The ministry sent about 7,500 regular troops to North America in peacetime. Colonists saw a standing army as costly and potentially tyrannical, while officials saw it as necessary against French Canadians, Native resistance, and westward settlers.
- British leaders increasingly treated Americans as colonial dependents rather than equal Britons. Colonists such as **John Dickinson** answered that they were British subjects “born to liberty.”

### New revenue and enforcement

- The **Revenue Act of 1762** tightened collection of older trade duties; the Royal Navy also targeted colonial trade with the French West Indies.
- **George Grenville**, prime minister after 1763, aimed to make imperial administration more efficient and to raise colonial revenue without relying on colonial assemblies.
- The **Sugar Act of 1764** lowered the old molasses duty but strengthened enforcement, expanded customs rules, and raised revenue from sugar, wine, coffee, and other imports.
- **Vice-admiralty courts** heard customs cases without a local jury. Colonists objected because accused smugglers could be tried far from home and because the courts weakened jury protections under English common law.
- The **Currency Act of 1764** restricted colonial paper money, helping British creditors but making debts and scarce hard money a greater problem for colonists.

## The Stamp Act crisis, pp. 157-160

- The **Stamp Act of 1765** required tax stamps on printed paper: legal documents, newspapers, pamphlets, licenses, dice, and playing cards. It was the first direct internal tax Parliament levied on the colonies.
- Grenville defended the tax through **virtual representation**: every member of Parliament supposedly represented the interests of all British subjects, including colonists who did not elect MPs.
- Colonists replied that legitimate taxation required actual representation in their own elected assemblies: “no taxation without representation.”
- The **Quartering Act of 1765** required colonial governments to supply British troops with barracks and provisions, adding to fears about a standing army and parliamentary control.

### Resistance and repeal

- The **Stamp Act Congress** (October 1765) brought delegates from nine colonies together to petition the king and Parliament. It accepted imperial ties but denied Parliament’s right to tax colonists without representation.
- The **Sons of Liberty**—urban artisans, merchants, laborers, and political leaders—used demonstrations, intimidation, and attacks on stamp distributors to stop enforcement.
- Colonial merchants organized nonimportation boycotts; because British manufacturers lost sales, they joined colonists in pressuring Parliament to repeal the act.
- **John Dickinson** and other Patriots drew on **English common law** and **natural rights**: rights such as liberty and property existed prior to government and could not be taken arbitrarily.
- Parliament repealed the Stamp Act in 1766 but passed the **Declaratory Act**, asserting its authority to legislate for the colonies “in all cases whatsoever.” Repeal ended the immediate crisis but left the sovereignty issue unresolved.

## The Townshend crisis and the West, pp. 160-167

### Townshend duties and organized resistance

- **Charles Townshend’s Townshend Act of 1767** put import duties on glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea. Townshend called these external trade duties, but colonists viewed their revenue purpose as the same unconstitutional taxation they had rejected under the Stamp Act.
- The revenue would pay royal governors and judges, making them less dependent on colonial assemblies; the act also strengthened the Board of Customs Commissioners in Boston.
- Colonists revived the **nonimportation movement**, pledging to buy fewer British manufactured goods. Women helped through homespun production and public agreements not to purchase imported luxuries.
- Britain sent troops to Boston in 1768 to protect customs officers and enforce imperial authority. The military presence intensified local anger.

### The problem of the West

- The **Proclamation of 1763** attempted to keep settlers east of the Appalachian Mountains and reserve western territory as Indian country. Britain hoped to prevent expensive frontier wars after Pontiac’s Rebellion.
- The policy frustrated land speculators, war veterans with land warrants, Indian traders, and ordinary squatters seeking farms. These groups treated western expansion as both economic opportunity and a right earned through imperial war.
- Native nations, including Shawnees, Delawares, and other Ohio Valley peoples, resisted settlement because it threatened their land and political autonomy. In 1770, leaders formed the **Scioto Confederacy** to oppose further expansion.
- Colonial secretary **Lord Hillsborough** favored making the Proclamation Line permanent to reduce military costs and avoid Native conflict. Westward settlers increasingly concluded that Britain would not protect their interests.

### Boston Massacre and sovereignty

- In March 1770, British soldiers fired into a tense Boston crowd and killed five townspeople. Patriots labeled the event the **Boston Massacre** and used images such as Paul Revere’s engraving to portray standing armies as instruments of tyranny.
- **Lord North** repealed most Townshend duties in 1770 after boycotts hurt British trade, but retained the tea duty to preserve Parliament’s claim of supremacy.
- By 1770, Patriots such as **Benjamin Franklin**, **Patrick Henry**, and **Samuel Adams** increasingly argued that colonial assemblies were equal to Parliament within the empire, sharing only the king. Governor **Thomas Hutchinson** rejected that argument: Parliament was either supreme or the colonies were independent.

## The road to independence, pp. 168-179

### Tea, coercion, and continental unity

- Patriots formed **committees of correspondence** in 1772-1773 to share information and coordinate responses to imperial threats. They transformed local protest into an intercolonial political network.
- The **Tea Act of May 1773** aided the debt-ridden East India Company by letting it sell tea directly in the colonies at a lower price. Patriots opposed it because buying the cheaper tea would accept the tea tax and because direct sales threatened colonial merchants with monopoly.
- At the **Boston Tea Party** (December 16, 1773), Patriots dumped 342 chests of tea into Boston Harbor rather than allow the tax to be collected.
- Parliament answered with the **Coercive Acts** (called the Intolerable Acts by Patriots): the Boston Port Bill closed the harbor; the Massachusetts Government Act curtailed the colony’s charter and town meetings; a new Quartering Act required barracks; and the Justice Act allowed certain trials to be moved outside Massachusetts.
- The **Quebec Act** was not designed as punishment, but its protection of Catholicism and expansion of Quebec into the Ohio Valley alarmed Protestants and western land seekers. Many colonists saw it as more evidence of arbitrary imperial rule.

### Continental Congress and armed conflict

- The **Continental Congress** met in Philadelphia in September 1774 with delegates from twelve mainland colonies. Delegates disagreed between compromise, union, and military preparation; Joseph Galloway’s plan for a colonial council under the crown failed narrowly.
- Congress demanded repeal of the Coercive Acts and created the **Continental Association**, a colony-wide boycott of British goods that also discouraged exports to Britain and the West Indies.
- Massachusetts Patriots organized provincial government and **minutemen**, militia members prepared to mobilize quickly. In April 1775, fighting at Lexington and Concord began when British troops tried to seize Patriot military supplies.
- The Second Continental Congress coordinated the war effort, appointed George Washington commander, and still sent the **Olive Branch Petition** seeking reconciliation. George III instead declared the colonies in rebellion, and Parliament’s Prohibitory Act cut off trade.

### Independence becomes the Patriot goal

- **Lord Dunmore**, Virginia’s royal governor, recruited Loyalists and in 1775 promised freedom to enslaved people and indentured servants who joined the British. The proclamation terrified slaveholders and pushed many southern whites toward independence.
- Patriot victories over Loyalists, including the Battle of Moore’s Creek Bridge in North Carolina (1776), weakened royal authority in the South.
- Western settlers occupied Kentucky after **Dunmore’s War**; **Daniel Boone** helped found Boonesborough. Their conflict with Native peoples and frustration with crown restrictions aligned many settlers with the Patriot cause.
- In January 1776, **Thomas Paine’s Common Sense** attacked monarchy and hereditary succession, argued that America should form independent republican states, and moved popular opinion toward a final break.
- On July 4, 1776, Congress approved the **Declaration of Independence**, drafted mainly by **Thomas Jefferson**. It accused George III of tyranny and grounded independence in natural rights, consent of the governed, and the right to replace destructive government.

## Essential chronology

- **1763:** Treaty of Paris ends the Great War for Empire; Proclamation of 1763 limits settlement west of the Appalachians.
- **1764:** Sugar Act and Currency Act.
- **1765:** Stamp Act, Quartering Act, Stamp Act Congress, and Sons of Liberty resistance.
- **1766:** Stamp Act repealed; Declaratory Act passed.
- **1767:** Townshend duties begin.
- **1768:** Troops arrive in Boston; nonimportation intensifies.
- **1770:** Boston Massacre; most Townshend duties repealed, tea tax retained.
- **1772-1773:** Committees of correspondence spread.
- **1773:** Tea Act; Boston Tea Party.
- **1774:** Coercive Acts and Quebec Act; First Continental Congress and Continental Association; Dunmore’s War.
- **April 1775:** Lexington and Concord; Second Continental Congress begins.
- **January 1776:** Common Sense published.
- **July 4, 1776:** Declaration of Independence adopted.

## Must-know terms and people

- **Sugar Act of 1764:** Revenue law that strengthened customs enforcement and taxed imported goods, including molasses.
- **Vice-admiralty courts:** Juryless courts used to enforce maritime and customs law.
- **Stamp Act of 1765:** Direct tax on printed materials in the colonies.
- **Virtual representation:** British claim that Parliament represented all subjects even when they did not elect its members.
- **Quartering Act of 1765:** Law requiring colonial support for British troops.
- **Stamp Act Congress:** 1765 intercolonial meeting that protested taxation without representation.
- **Sons of Liberty:** Patriot organization that mobilized popular resistance to British taxes.
- **English common law:** Legal tradition protecting rights such as trial by jury and limits on arbitrary power.
- **Natural rights:** Inherent rights—especially life, liberty, and property—that government must protect.
- **Declaratory Act of 1766:** Parliamentary assertion of authority over the colonies in all cases.
- **Townshend Act of 1767:** Import duties intended to raise colonial revenue and strengthen royal officials.
- **Nonimportation movement:** Boycott of British goods used as economic pressure.
- **Committees of correspondence:** Intercolonial communication networks that coordinated Patriot action.
- **Tea Act of May 1773:** Law aiding the East India Company while retaining the tax on tea.
- **Coercive Acts:** Punitive 1774 laws targeting Massachusetts after the Tea Party.
- **Continental Congress:** Delegates from the colonies who coordinated resistance and later the war for independence.
- **Continental Association:** First Continental Congress’s 1774 boycott agreement.
- **Dunmore’s War:** 1774 conflict in the Ohio Valley that opened Kentucky settlement while escalating Native-colonial conflict.
- **Minutemen:** Rapid-response Patriot militia.
- **Second Continental Congress:** Body that managed the colonial war effort and declared independence.
- **Declaration of Independence:** 1776 justification for independence based on natural rights and consent.
- **Popular sovereignty:** Principle that ultimate political authority rests with the people.
- **George Grenville:** Prime minister who sponsored the Sugar and Stamp Acts.
- **John Dickinson:** Pennsylvania lawyer and Patriot who defended colonial liberties and initially favored reconciliation.
- **Charles Townshend:** Chancellor who sponsored the 1767 duties.
- **Lord North:** Prime minister who repealed most Townshend duties but retained the tea tax.
- **Samuel Adams:** Massachusetts radical Patriot who helped organize committees and resistance to the Tea Act.
- **Lord Dunmore:** Virginia governor whose offer of freedom to enslaved people who joined Britain radicalized southern Patriots.
- **Thomas Paine:** Author of Common Sense, the widely read argument for republican independence.
- **Thomas Jefferson:** Principal author of the Declaration of Independence.

## Likely quiz comparisons

- **Stamp Act vs. Townshend duties:** The Stamp Act was a direct internal tax on printed paper; Townshend duties were import taxes. Colonists resisted both because each raised revenue without their elected representation.
- **Virtual representation vs. colonial representation:** Parliament said members represented all British subjects; colonists said only representatives they elected in their own assemblies could tax them.
- **Stamp Act resistance vs. Tea Act resistance:** Both used boycotts and crowd action, but the Tea Act controversy focused on Parliament’s retained tea tax and the East India Company’s monopoly advantage.
- **First Continental Congress vs. Second Continental Congress:** The first organized economic resistance and petitions in 1774; the second managed armed conflict in 1775 and ultimately declared independence in 1776.
- **British western policy vs. settler goals:** Britain wanted the Proclamation Line to limit costs and Native conflict; speculators, veterans, and settlers wanted access to western land.
- **Patriot constitutional argument vs. independence argument:** Early Patriots demanded rights within the empire; by 1776 they argued that a tyrannical king had forfeited authority and that independent republican government was necessary.
