## Key concepts and events

### Markets and government-assisted growth

**Neomercantilist (p. 250)**  
A system of **government-assisted economic development** that promoted private property, markets, and individual opportunity. Republican state legislatures supported banks, manufacturing, and transportation rather than leaving development entirely to private enterprise. Unlike colonial mercantilism directed toward enriching Britain, this policy aimed to strengthen the American economy and improve the public welfare. The Commonwealth System was its practical expression.

**Panic of 1819 (p. 251)**  
A major economic downturn caused by risky banking and lending combined with falling agricultural prices after the Napoleonic Wars. Banks had issued notes without enough gold and silver reserves and lent money for overpriced land; when farmers' incomes fell, they could not repay their debts. **Falling crop prices → debt defaults → bank failures and collapsing land values.** The panic showed the risks of dependence on national and international markets and the boom-and-bust business cycle.

**Commonwealth System (p. 256)**  
A state-government policy that gave aid and legal privileges to private businesses whose projects were supposed to improve the **general welfare**. Legislatures granted corporate charters, monopolies, and powers such as eminent domain to support transportation and economic development. Supporters argued that roads, bridges, and productive businesses benefited the whole community. Critics argued that special privileges violated equal rights and gave corporations too much power.

### Marriage, motherhood, and family size

**Sentimentalism (p. 258)**  
A cultural outlook, connected to the European Romantic movement, that emphasized **feeling and emotional experience** rather than the Enlightenment's emphasis on reason. After 1800, it influenced American literature, theater, religious preaching, and courtship. By encouraging people to value affection and emotional compatibility, sentimentalism helped make love-based marriage more desirable than marriages arranged mainly for property or family advantage.

**Companionate marriages (p. 258)**  
Marriages chosen for **affection, intimacy, and companionship**, rather than primarily for economic interest or parental approval. As parents' control weakened and sentimentalism spread, more young adults selected their own spouses. These voluntary partnerships fit republican ideals of individual choice and, in theory, equality. In practice, husbands still held legal control over family property and usually dominated the household, so emotional companionship did not guarantee equal rights.

**Demographic transition (p. 259)**  
In this chapter, the sharp **decline in birthrates** that began in the northern states after 1800. Young men's westward migration left more eastern women unmarried or marrying later, reducing the number of children they bore. Urban middle-class couples also deliberately limited births so fathers could provide adequate inheritances and mothers could pursue interests beyond continuous child rearing. Smaller families changed women's lives and the attention parents could devote to each child.

**Republican motherhood (p. 259)**  
The idea that mothers served the republic by raising **virtuous, informed citizens**, especially sons who understood liberty and government. Benjamin Rush argued that educated women should guide their husbands' morality and teach their children republican principles. This gave women's education and child rearing public importance, even while most women remained excluded from voting and political office. It expanded women's influence through the family without establishing political equality.

### Slavery, racial exclusion, and sectional conflict

**Manumission (p. 265)**  
The freeing of an enslaved person by their owner, sometimes after the person purchased their freedom. Revolutionary ideals, Quaker antislavery activism, and early Methodist and Baptist criticism of slavery encouraged some owners to grant freedom. Virginia's 1782 manumission law allowed owners to free enslaved people, and thousands gained liberty. **Manumission was individual liberation; gradual emancipation laws were broader state policies**, often requiring years of continued labor before freedom.

**Herrenvolk republic (p. 266)**  
A republic that restricted liberty and legal equality to whites while preserving the enslavement and subordination of Black people; *herrenvolk* means **"master race."** Southern leaders used this racial definition of republicanism to defend white supremacy and slaveholders' property. Fear after Gabriel Prosser's planned rebellion in 1800 helped close debate over emancipation. The system exposed the contradiction between republican claims of freedom and an economy built on human bondage.

**American Colonization Society (p. 267)**  
An organization founded in 1817 that promoted freeing enslaved people and **resettling them outside the United States**, especially in its West African colony of Liberia. Supporters such as Henry Clay argued that slavery hindered economic progress but feared that emancipation without removal would cause racial conflict. Most free Black Americans opposed colonization because they regarded the United States as their homeland and claimed citizenship there. The cotton boom strengthened slaveholders' commitment to slavery, and the society's limited resettlement failed to solve the problem of bondage.

**Missouri Compromise (p. 269)**  
A series of agreements associated with Henry Clay that settled the Missouri statehood crisis of 1819-1821. **Maine entered as a free state in 1820; Missouri entered as a slave state in 1821**, preserving the balance between free and slave states in the Senate. Slavery was prohibited in the rest of the Louisiana Purchase **north of 36°30′**, with Missouri as the exception. The compromise preserved the Union temporarily but showed how slavery's westward expansion had become a dangerous sectional dispute.

### Religious freedom and revival

**Established church (p. 270)**  
A church with official legal recognition and government support, traditionally including compulsory religious taxes. After independence, Enlightenment arguments and dissenting Protestant groups challenged these privileges. Virginia's 1786 religious-freedom law made churches equal before the law and gave direct financial support to none. Disestablishment weakened government control of religion, although religious restrictions and indirect support such as tax exemptions continued in many states.

**Voluntarism (p. 270)**  
The funding of churches by their **members' voluntary contributions**, rather than compulsory government taxes. It let ordinary church members exercise more control over ministers and reduced the danger of state interference. Supporters connected it to freedom of conscience, self-government, and republican principles. Remember the contrast: **establishment relied on official privilege and compulsory support; voluntarism relied on members' choice and support.**

**Unchurched (p. 270)**  
Americans who lacked active church membership or regular participation in organized religion. Methodist and Baptist evangelists attracted many of these people through accessible preaching, emotional worship, communal singing, and religious fellowship. Revivals and traveling preachers also helped migrants build social ties in new communities. Their conversion contributed to the rapid growth of evangelical denominations.

**Second Great Awakening (p. 271)**  
A decades-long wave of Protestant religious revivals beginning in the 1790s. Methodist and Baptist preachers used plain, emotional preaching, traveling circuits, and frontier camp meetings; the **Cane Ridge revival in Kentucky in 1801** was a major example. Revivalism emphasized spiritual renewal and, increasingly, people's ability to choose salvation and improve themselves. It expanded evangelical churches, encouraged independent Black religious institutions, and drew women into charitable work, education, and reform, although spiritual equality did not consistently produce racial or gender equality.

## Key people

**John Jacob Astor (p. 250)**  
A German immigrant merchant who became one of America's first millionaires. He made money trading Pacific Northwest furs in China and investing in New York City real estate. His career illustrates the wealth available through international commerce, access to capital, and an expanding market economy in the early republic.

**Benjamin Rush (p. 259)**  
A Philadelphia physician who argued in *Thoughts on Female Education* (1787) that educated women should guide their families' morality and teach sons the principles of liberty and government. His argument helped define **republican motherhood**. It justified female education through women's responsibilities to the republic as mothers, rather than through equal political rights.

**Henry Clay (p. 267)**  
Speaker of the House, a Kentucky slaveholder, and a member of the American Colonization Society. He argued that slavery impeded economic development but favored emancipation combined with removal of Black people from the United States. He also helped arrange the **Missouri Compromise**, preserving the Senate's sectional balance. His role here links colonization, the defense of the Union, and compromise over slavery's expansion.

**Richard Allen (p. 267)**  
A formerly enslaved Methodist minister who purchased his freedom and helped create independent Black religious institutions. In 1816, he became the first bishop of the **African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church**. He opposed colonization, arguing that Black Americans had built the country through their labor and suffering and had a right to remain as citizens. His leadership connected religious independence with resistance to racial exclusion.

**Lyman Beecher (p. 273)**  
A prominent Congregationalist minister who retained the belief that humans tended toward sin but rejected Calvinist predestination and affirmed people's ability to **choose God**. His emphasis on free will reflected the Awakening's growing belief that individuals could shape their own destiny. This outlook supported the wider religious movement toward personal improvement and benevolent social reform.

**Emma Willard (p. 276)**  
An early advocate of higher education for women who opened the Middlebury Female Seminary in Vermont in 1814 and later founded girls' academies in Waterford and Troy, New York. Such schools expanded women's access to intellectual and moral instruction. Educated women increasingly became public-school teachers, gaining an acknowledged role in public life, although their pay remained lower than men's.
