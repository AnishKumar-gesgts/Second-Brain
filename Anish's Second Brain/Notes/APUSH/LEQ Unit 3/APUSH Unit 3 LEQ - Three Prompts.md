# APUSH Unit 3 LEQ - Three Prompts

These are **terms to understand**, not an essay to memorize. For each one, know **when**, **what was happening**, and **how it helps answer the exact prompt**. The background terms belong in an introduction; the evidence terms can support body paragraphs. Choose the strongest examples rather than trying to use every row.

Source notes: [[APUSH Unit 3 - pp. 152-179]], [[APUSH Chapter 6 - Making War and Republican Governments 1776-1789]], and [[APUSH Chapter 7 - Key Terms]].

## Prompt 1 — Why did Americans rebel in 1776?

> Evaluate the relative importance of Parliamentary taxation, British military measures, restriction of civil liberties, and the legacy of colonial religious and political ideas as factors prompting Americans to rebel in 1776.

### Background terms

| Term and time frame | Context | Why it sets up this prompt |
|---|---|---|
| **Seven Years' War / French and Indian War (1756–1763)** | Victory left Britain with debt, new North American territory, and an army to support. | Explains Britain's push for colonial revenue and tighter control after 1763. |
| **Proclamation of 1763** | Britain tried to limit settlement west of the Appalachians to reduce frontier conflict and expense. | Adds western land restrictions to the setting; taxation was not the only colonial grievance. |
| **English common law and colonial assemblies (before 1763)** | Colonists expected jury protections and meaningful authority for their elected local assemblies. | Establishes the older liberties against which colonists judged new taxes, courts, and imperial controls. |
| **First Great Awakening (1730s–1740s)** | Revival movements encouraged religious choice and challenged some established religious authorities. | Provides limited background for the prompt's *religious ideas* category. Do not claim it directly caused independence or outweighs the specific political and imperial conflicts below. |

### Terms for answering the question

| Term and time frame                                                                                    | Context                                                                                                                                                            | How it answers the prompt                                                                                                                                                                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ==**Sugar Act and vice-admiralty courts (1764)**==                                                     | Britain raised customs revenue and used maritime courts without local juries for customs cases.                                                                    | Links **taxation** to complaints about **legal rights**; enforcement made a revenue policy look like an attack on English liberties.                                                                 |
| ==**Stamp Act, **==**virtual representation, and Stamp Act Congress (1765)**                           | Parliament taxed printed materials; Britain said MPs represented all subjects, while colonial delegates denied taxation without their own elected representatives. | The clearest example that the central tax issue was **political authority**, not simply the amount charged.                                                                                          |
| ==**Declaratory Act (1766)**==                                                                         | Parliament repealed the Stamp Act but asserted power to legislate for the colonies “in all cases whatsoever.”                                                      | Shows why repeal did not settle the underlying dispute over sovereignty.                                                                                                                             |
| **Townshend duties and nonimportation (1767–1770)**                                                    | Britain taxed imports partly to pay royal officials; colonists boycotted British goods.                                                                            | Even an import duty threatened colonial assemblies when it made governors and judges less dependent on them.                                                                                         |
| ==**Quartering Act and Boston Massacre (1765; 1770)**==                                                | Colonies were required to support troops; soldiers stationed in Boston later killed five people amid street tensions.                                              | Gives concrete evidence for the **military measures** factor and fear of a standing army.                                                                                                            |
| **Committees of correspondence (1772–1773)**                                                           | Colonists shared reports of British actions and coordinated resistance across colonies.                                                                            | Shows how rights-based arguments and local grievances became an intercolonial political movement.                                                                                                    |
| **Tea Act, Boston Tea Party, and Coercive Acts (1773–1774)**                                           | Patriots resisted tea sold with Parliament's tax still in place; Britain then closed Boston's port and curtailed Massachusetts self-government.                    | Connects **taxation** to a direct **restriction of local liberties**, turning a Massachusetts dispute into a wider crisis.                                                                           |
| **First Continental Congress and Continental Association (1774)**                                      | Delegates coordinated petitions and a broad boycott in response to British policies.                                                                               | Shows that colonists first sought collective resistance and redress, rather than immediately declaring independence.                                                                                 |
| ==**Lexington and Concord (1775)**==                                                                   | British troops and Massachusetts militia fought when troops moved to seize Patriot supplies.                                                                       | Armed conflict made reconciliation harder and gave military coercion greater immediate importance.                                                                                                   |
| ==**Common Sense; natural rights and popular sovereignty in the Declaration of Independence (1776)**== | Paine attacked monarchy; the Declaration argued that government depends on consent and can be replaced when it violates rights.                                    | **Political ideas** converted resistance to particular policies into a justification for independence. The Declaration explains the final decision; earlier events explain how colonists reached it. |

**Relative-importance judgment to practice:** Taxation exposed the dispute over who could govern; military action and restrictions on self-government made the crisis urgent; political ideas justified separation. Religious ideas can be mentioned as background, but these notes give them much weaker direct evidence as a cause of rebellion.

## Prompt 2 — Did the new government face the same problems as Britain?

> Between 1783 and 1800, the new U.S. government faced the same political and economic issues that troubled Britain's relations with the colonies before the Revolution. Assess the validity of this generalization.

### Background terms

| Term and time frame | Context | Why it sets up this prompt |
|---|---|---|
| **Stamp Act and colonial representation dispute (1765)** | Parliament needed revenue; colonists argued that a government they did not elect could not tax them. | Supplies the **before** side of the comparison: revenue needs versus consent and representation. |
| **Coercive Acts (1774)** | Britain punished Massachusetts and limited its self-government after the Tea Party. | Supplies a second **before** issue: fear that a government enforcing order could violate local liberties. |
| **Treaty of Paris (1783)** | Britain recognized U.S. independence, but the new republic still faced war debt, trade problems, and arguments over government power. | Marks the transition to the prompt's 1783–1800 period and explains why independence did not end these disputes. |

### Terms for answering the question

| Term and time frame                                                              | Context                                                                                                                                               | How it answers the prompt                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| ==**Articles of Confederation (1781–1789)**==                                    | Congress could request funds but could not directly tax, regulate trade effectively, or compel state compliance.                                      | ==Fear of concentrated authority continued==, but the new government now suffered from **too little** fiscal power.                                                                                          |
| ==**Shays's Rebellion (1786–1787)**==                                            | Massachusetts farmers resisted **state** hard-money taxes, debts, and foreclosures by closing courts.                                                 | Like earlier colonial protesters, ==citizens challenged burdens they considered unfair==; unlike the colonial case, the taxing officials were Americans. Do not attribute Massachusetts's taxes to Congress. |
| **Constitutional Convention and Federalists versus Antifederalists (1787–1788)** | The Constitution gave the federal government taxing and commerce powers; critics feared distant rule, a standing army, and elite control.             | Shows that the old question of **effective government versus liberty** survived independence, now within a debate over an American constitution.                                                             |
| ==**Bill of Rights (1791)**==                                                    | The first ten amendments protected speech, religion, and legal rights after Antifederalists demanded explicit limits.                                 | A key **difference** from imperial rule: Americans wrote ==protections== into the new federal framework.                                                                                                     |
| **Hamilton's Report on the Public Credit (1790)**                                | Hamilton proposed federal assumption of state war debts and full repayment of securities; critics worried about speculators and creditors benefiting. | Debt and revenue remained contentious, but Americans now disputed **who paid and who profited** from their own government's policy.                                                                          |
| **Bank of the United States (1791)**                                             | Hamilton defended the bank through implied federal powers; Jefferson and Madison argued the Constitution did not authorize it.                        | Demonstrates a new version of the old authority dispute: how broadly could a national government interpret its powers?                                                                                       |
| ==**Whiskey Rebellion (1794)**==                                                 | Western Pennsylvania farmers resisted a federal excise tax; Washington raised a militia and the resistance dispersed.                                 | Strongest direct comparison with pre-Revolutionary tax protest. It also shows that the U.S. government could enforce a tax where the Articles government could not.                                          |
| ==**Naturalization, Alien, and Sedition Acts (1798)**==                          | Federalists restricted immigration and punished certain published attacks on the president or Congress amid conflict with France.                     | Revived fears that ==officials could restrict liberties and silence opposition.==                                                                                                                            |
| **Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (1798)**                                     | Madison and Jefferson urged state challenges to the 1798 acts as unconstitutional.                                                                    | Shows a distinctly **American constitutional remedy** and a new dispute over who could judge federal power.                                                                                                  |

**Validity judgment to practice:** The generalization is substantially valid for debt, taxes, power, and civil liberties. It needs qualification because post-Revolutionary conflicts took place under elected governments, a written Constitution, and a Bill of Rights.

## Prompt 3 — How radical was the American Revolution?

> Analyze the extent to which the American Revolution represented a radical alteration in American political ideas and institutions.

### Background terms

| Term and time frame | Context | Why it sets up this prompt |
|---|---|---|
| **Monarchy and colonial assemblies (before 1776)** | Colonists elected local assemblies but lived under a hereditary king and imperial authority. | Establishes the political baseline: the Revolution changed the **source** of authority, while building on an older practice of representative government. |
| **Natural rights, consent, and the Declaration of Independence (1776)** | The Declaration held that people possess rights and may replace a government that violates them. | Introduces the revolutionary political **idea** against which later institutions and exclusions can be measured. |
| **Revolutionary War and Treaty of Paris (1775–1783)** | The war ended British rule; the 1783 treaty recognized U.S. independence. | Establishes the opportunity to construct new governments, while U.S. western claims ignored Native consent. |

### Terms for answering the question

| Term and time frame                                                            | Context                                                                                                                                         | How it answers the prompt                                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ==**State constitutions and Pennsylvania constitution (1776 onward)**==        | States wrote republican constitutions; Pennsylvania adopted broad male suffrage, annual elections, one legislative house, and a weak executive. | Strong evidence of a **radical institutional shift** toward elected government, especially for many white men. Other states kept property qualifications. |
| ==**Articles of Confederation (1781–1789)**==                                  | Sovereign states joined in a republic without a monarch, while the national Congress had limited power.                                         | Shows a new form of government based on states and representatives, though its weakness prompted further redesign.                                        |
| **Northern gradual emancipation and continuing southern slavery (late 1700s)** | Some northern states began ending slavery gradually; slavery remained entrenched in the South.                                                  | Revolutionary liberty challenged slavery unevenly, making the degree of change different across regions and populations.                                  |
| **Northwest Ordinance (1787)**                                                 | Set a path for western territories to become equal states and barred slavery in the Northwest Territory.                                        | Innovative republican expansion, but one that presumed U.S. authority over land Native peoples occupied.                                                  |
| ==**Constitutional Convention and Great Compromise (1787)**==                  | Delegates created a stronger federal government, with population-based House representation and equal state representation in the Senate.       | Major change in **institutional design**, while preserving representative republican government rather than giving all people equal political power.      |
| **Three-Fifths Compromise (1787)**                                             | The Constitution counted enslaved people for representation and taxation without granting them political rights.                                | Direct evidence that a new republican institution also preserved and amplified an older racial hierarchy.                                                 |
| ==**Federalist No. 10 and Antifederalist objections (1787–1788)**==            | Madison argued a large republic could control factions; opponents feared distant power and demanded rights protections.                         | Shows that Americans were actively redefining what a republic could be and debating whether its new institutions protected liberty.                       |
| **Judith Sargent Murray and republican motherhood (late 1780s–1790s)**         | Murray defended women's intellectual equality; the ideal of republican motherhood emphasized women's role in raising virtuous citizens.         | Revolutionary ideas broadened arguments about women's civic importance, but women did not gain voting rights.                                             |
| ==**Bill of Rights (1791)**==                                                  | The first ten amendments limited federal power and protected specified individual liberties.                                                    | A lasting institutional change in rights protection, though protected liberties did not amount to equal political participation for everyone.             |
| **Treaty of Greenville (1795)**                                                | Following Native resistance and U.S. military victory, Native leaders ceded extensive Ohio lands.                                               | Shows that the expanding republic's claims to self-government coexisted with denial of Native sovereignty.                                                |

**Extent judgment to practice:** The Revolution was most radical in **where legitimate power came from** and **how governments were built**. Its immediate change was much narrower for enslaved people, women, and Native nations.
