# APUSH Unit 3 LEQ - Three Prompts

**Source boundary:** Prompt 1 draws its pre-1776 evidence from [[APUSH Unit 3 - pp. 152-179]]. Prompts 2 and 3 use [[APUSH Chapter 6 - Making War and Republican Governments 1776-1789]] and [[APUSH Chapter 7 - Key Terms]]. Named examples below are limited to terms and events in those notes.

**Rubric target:** Background/context (1), argumentative thesis with reasons (1), specific evidence (2: aim for six well-explained examples in two or three body paragraphs), and analysis/significance (2). For each example, state **who/what, when, and where**, then explain **why it proves your claim**. Keep events in chronological order within each argument.

## Prompt 1 - Why Americans rebelled in 1776

> Evaluate the relative importance of Parliamentary taxation, British military measures, restriction of civil liberties, and the legacy of colonial religious and political ideas as factors prompting Americans to rebel in 1776.

### Background/context

The **Seven Years' War (1756-1763)** left Britain with heavy debt, more territory to administer, and troops to support in North America. British ministers expected colonists to help pay those costs. Colonists, however, expected their own assemblies and the protections of **English common law** to limit imperial power. The **Proclamation of 1763** also frustrated western settlers by restricting expansion. Those fiscal and political tensions formed the setting for resistance to new laws after 1763.

### Sample thesis

**Parliamentary taxation and British coercion were the most important causes of rebellion because taxes challenged colonial self-government, military measures and curtailed liberties turned resistance into a continental crisis, and rights-based political ideas—reinforced more modestly by religious fears—justified independence.**

### Six-example body plan - three chronological paragraphs

**Body 1: Taxation turns an imperial debt problem into a sovereignty dispute (1765-1767).**

1. **Stamp Act (1765):** Parliament taxed colonial printed materials directly. The **Stamp Act Congress** rejected taxation without elected colonial representation, while Britain claimed **virtual representation**. Explain that the decisive issue was *who had authority to tax*, not just the cost of the stamps.
2. **Townshend Act (1767):** Import duties on common goods raised revenue to pay royal governors and judges, making those officials less dependent on colonial assemblies. Colonists revived **nonimportation**. This shows why even an external duty threatened local political control.

**Body 2: Enforcement and lost liberties broaden resistance (1770-1774).**

3. **Boston Massacre (1770):** British troops stationed in Boston after customs protests fired into a crowd, killing five. Patriots used the event to depict the standing army as a danger to colonial liberty. Military enforcement made arguments about tyranny seem immediate.
4. **Tea Act and Boston Tea Party (1773) -> Coercive Acts (1774):** Patriots refused taxed East India Company tea and destroyed it in Boston Harbor. Parliament then closed the port and curtailed Massachusetts self-government, including town meetings. These punishments turned a Massachusetts dispute into a continental defense of local liberties.

**Body 3: Beliefs and armed conflict turn resistance into independence (1774-1776).**

5. **Quebec Act (1774):** Britain's protection of Catholicism in Quebec and extension of Quebec into the Ohio Valley alarmed many Protestant colonists and western land seekers. It was not written to punish Massachusetts, but colonists interpreted it alongside the Coercive Acts as another sign of arbitrary imperial rule. This supports a **secondary** role for religious fear.
6. **Common Sense (1776) and Declaration of Independence (1776):** Thomas Paine attacked monarchy and argued for republican independence; the Declaration grounded separation in **natural rights**, popular sovereignty, and consent. These ideas supplied a reason to replace imperial rule rather than merely repeal individual laws. Fighting at **Lexington and Concord (1775)** had already made reconciliation harder.

### Key terms by factor - use them to deepen, not crowd, the essay

| Factor | Relevant terms in the Unit 3 note | How to use them |
|---|---|---|
| Parliamentary taxation | **Sugar Act, Stamp Act, virtual representation, Stamp Act Congress, Declaratory Act, Townshend Act, nonimportation, Tea Act** | Show the shift from objecting to individual taxes toward rejecting Parliament's claimed supremacy. The Declaratory Act asserted authority even when the Stamp Act was repealed. |
| British military measures | **Quartering Act, Boston Massacre, minutemen, Lexington and Concord** | Trace growing fear of a standing army and the move from protest to armed conflict. |
| Restrictions on civil liberties | **Vice-admiralty courts, English common law, Coercive Acts, Continental Congress, Continental Association** | Explain complaints about jury protections, town meetings, and self-government, then show how colonies coordinated resistance. |
| Colonial religious and political ideas | **Natural rights, popular sovereignty, John Dickinson, committees of correspondence, Quebec Act, Thomas Paine, Common Sense, Declaration of Independence** | Explain the language and networks that turned grievances into a case for independence. The Quebec Act demonstrates religious alarm, but the notes offer stronger evidence for political than religious ideas. |
| Western pressure, if useful for qualification | **Proclamation of 1763, Dunmore's War, Lord Dunmore** | Western land restrictions and Dunmore's promise of freedom to people escaping Patriot slavery pushed different groups toward independence for different reasons. These factors complicate a taxation-only argument. |

### Analysis and significance

- **Rank the causes:** Taxation exposed the sovereignty dispute; military force and the Coercive Acts made compromise less plausible; rights-based ideas justified a final break. Religious alarm mattered, but the allowed notes do not show it as the primary cause.
- **Show change over time:** Protest over laws in 1765 became intercolonial organization in 1774, armed conflict in 1775, and an argument for independence in 1776.
- **Avoid a false shortcut:** The **Declaration of Independence** explains the Patriots' final justification; use earlier acts and confrontations to prove what moved them toward that decision.

## Prompt 2 - Did the new government face the same problems as Britain?

> Between 1783 and 1800, the new U.S. government faced the same political and economic issues that troubled Britain's relations with the colonies before the Revolution. Assess the validity of this generalization.

### Background/context

The **Treaty of Paris (1783)** made the United States independent, but independence did not settle questions about taxation, public debt, trade, representation, or the limits of government power. The **Articles of Confederation** initially left most authority with the states. Fiscal disorder and **Shays's Rebellion** then helped drive the move to a stronger Constitution. As that government exercised power in the 1790s, Americans again argued over taxes and civil liberties.

### Sample thesis

**The generalization is mostly valid because postwar debt and taxes revived struggles over fiscal authority, the Alien and Sedition Acts renewed fears of arbitrary power, and constitutional rights and elected institutions made the new government's conflicts less like imperial rule.**

### Six-example body plan - two paragraphs

**Body 1: Economic burdens and the power to govern (1783-1794).**

1. **Articles of Confederation:** Congress could request funds but could not tax or regulate trade effectively. This shows that fear of distant fiscal authority survived independence, but it also made the new government financially weak.
2. **Shays's Rebellion (1786-1787):** Massachusetts farmers shut courts amid hard-money taxes, debt, and foreclosure pressure. Like earlier protests against imposed burdens, this challenged officials who seemed unresponsive; unlike the colonial case, the officials were Americans.
3. **Report on the Public Credit (1790):** Alexander Hamilton proposed federal assumption of state war debts and full payment of securities. The dispute over who paid and who profited shows that revenue and representation remained contentious under the Constitution.
4. **Whiskey Rebellion (1794):** Western Pennsylvania farmers resisted a federal excise tax. Washington's militia response proved that the new government could enforce a tax; it also renewed the question of whose economic interests national policy served.

**Body 2: Rights, opposition, and constitutional remedies (1787-1800).**

5. **Bill of Rights (ratified 1791):** Promised during the Federalist-Antifederalist debate, it protected speech, religion, and legal rights. Its adoption shows Americans knew a stronger government could threaten liberties and tried to set explicit limits.
6. **Naturalization, Alien, and Sedition Acts (1798) -> Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (1798):** Federalists restricted immigration and punished some opposition writing; Republicans argued these measures violated constitutional limits. The conflict resembles older fears of arbitrary power, while the resolutions show a specifically American dispute over which part of the federal system could judge a law.

### Analysis and significance

- **Continuity:** Independence did not erase conflict between governments needing revenue and citizens demanding consent, fair treatment, and protected dissent.
- **Change:** The dispute took place under state and federal constitutions with elected representatives. The **Bill of Rights** and the Republican response to the 1798 acts supplied new ways to challenge authority.
- **Qualification:** Do not claim the Articles imposed the taxes that provoked Shays's Rebellion; **Massachusetts** imposed them, while the Confederation's fiscal weakness formed part of the wider crisis.

### Additional relevant key terms from the two notes

| Key terms | Why they fit |
|---|---|
| **Constitutional Convention, Virginia Plan, New Jersey Plan, Great Compromise** | The 1787 debate over large- and small-state representation shows Americans rebuilding political authority after the Articles proved too weak. |
| **Federalists, Antifederalists, James Madison** | Federalists defended effective national power; Antifederalists feared taxes, a standing army, and elite rule. Madison's defense of the Constitution helps explain the answer to those fears. |

These terms support the **U.S. side** of the comparison. Specific pre-Revolutionary British policies would require evidence from earlier notes, which this file is restricted from using.

## Prompt 3 - How radical was the American Revolution?

> Analyze the extent to which the American Revolution represented a radical alteration in American political ideas and institutions.

### Background/context

The war for independence broke imperial rule. Americans then had to decide what republican government meant in state constitutions and in their first national framework. Wartime financial strain, western settlement, and conflict over who counted as a political member tested the reach of revolutionary ideals. The question asks for **degree of change**: compare new ideas and institutions with the limits that remained.

### Sample thesis

**The Revolution was radical in government but limited in political inclusion because state constitutions and the Articles replaced imperial rule, the Constitution and Bill of Rights reshaped national authority, and slavery, limits on women's participation, and disregard for Native sovereignty preserved older hierarchies.**

### Six-example body plan - two paragraphs

**Body 1: Independence and the first republican governments (1776-1783).**

1. **Pennsylvania constitution of 1776:** A single-house legislature, broad male suffrage, annual elections, and a weak executive put more trust in ordinary white male voters than older elite-centered systems did. This is strong evidence for a radical change in political authority.
2. **Articles of Confederation:** States joined in a republic without a monarch and retained sovereignty. The framework was new, but its weak taxing and enforcement powers exposed a problem with protecting independence.
3. **Treaty of Paris (1783):** Britain recognized U.S. independence and granted the United States claims west to the Mississippi without Native consent. The republic gained political sovereignty while disregarding Native sovereignty, an early limit on its vision of self-government.

**Body 2: Reforming the republic, while preserving inequality (1787-1791).**

4. **Constitutional Convention and Great Compromise (1787):** Delegates replaced the Articles with a stronger government and balanced population-based representation in the House with equal state representation in the Senate. This was a major institutional revision, not a rejection of republican government.
5. **Three-Fifths Compromise (1787):** The Constitution counted enslaved people for representation while denying them political rights and preserving slavery. This is direct evidence that new institutions could reinforce an older hierarchy.
6. **Bill of Rights (ratified 1791):** The first ten amendments set explicit limits on federal power and protected individual rights, answering Antifederalist worries about the new Constitution. Their protections did not give everyone equal political participation.

### Analysis and significance

- **Women:** [[APUSH Chapter 6 - Making War and Republican Governments 1776-1789|Chapter 6]] notes **Judith Sargent Murray** and **republican motherhood**. Murray challenged claims of women's intellectual inferiority, but women did not gain voting rights. Use this as an additional example if a third body paragraph helps.
- **Native nations:** The **Treaty of Paris (1783)** assigned western territory to the United States without Native consent; the **Treaty of Greenville (1795)** followed Native military resistance and ceded more land. These examples show that republican self-government for settlers could coexist with the denial of Native sovereignty.
- **Judgment:** The change was most radical in the **basis and design of government**, less radical in **who could participate and whose freedom the government protected**. Keep returning to that comparison rather than listing reforms and exclusions separately.

### Additional relevant key terms from the two notes

| Key terms | Why they fit |
|---|---|
| **Federalists, Antifederalists, James Madison, Federalist No. 10** | Debates about the Constitution and factions show that republicanism changed form and remained contested. |
| **Virginia Plan, New Jersey Plan** | Their competing models of representation led to the Great Compromise. |
| **Northwest Ordinance of 1787** | Offered western territories a path to equal statehood while excluding slavery north of the Ohio River; it also presumed U.S. control over Native land. |
| **Philipsburg Proclamation** | Britain's offer of freedom to enslaved people who fled Patriot owners reveals how the war's promise of liberty was unevenly distributed. |
| **Judith Sargent Murray, republican motherhood** | Murray argued for women's intellectual equality, while the prevailing republican ideal gave women a civic role without voting rights. |
| **Treaty of Greenville** | Native resistance and the 1795 land cession show whose sovereignty the expanding republic failed to respect. |

Choose terms that serve the thesis; do not try to fit every one into a 40-minute essay.

## Fast essay check

1. **Introduction:** Give two or three sentences of specific background and end with a thesis that answers *relative importance*, *validity*, or *extent*.
2. **Body paragraphs:** Start each with a reason from the thesis. Use three concrete examples per paragraph when possible; explain each example's connection to the reason.
3. **Analysis:** Add a comparison, change-over-time point, or qualification, then state why it affects your judgment.
4. **Chronology:** Keep examples in order within each paragraph, and avoid using an event after 1776 as direct evidence of why rebellion began before 1776.
